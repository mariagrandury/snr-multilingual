\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:methodology}
We study the problem setting in which a developer chooses between different design choices in language model pretraining.
We consider choices related to model architecture, optimization, and multilingual data composition, and study them across different model sizes and training checkpoints.
The goal is to determine whether the ranking of models that differ in a design choice (model variants) at smaller sizes or earlier checkpoints is preserved at the largest model size or after full training.
We first describe the model ladder, comprising model variants with different design choices (\S\ref{subsec:models}) and the evaluation protocol used to rank them (\S\ref{subsec:evaluation}). We then define ranking agreement, which measures how consistently these rankings are preserved across model sizes and training checkpoints (\S\ref{subsec:methodology-da}).

\subsection{Model Ladder}
\label{subsec:models}

We construct a controlled multilingual ladder of \nmodels models to measure ranking agreement across model size, training progress, and pretraining design choices.
%\textbf{Number of parameters $N$.}
We train seven model sizes, measured in non-embedding parameters: 90M, 175M, 350M, 600M, 1B, 1.7B, 3B. We use the models between 90M to 1B parameters to predict performance at the 1.7B scale, and evaluate whether these findings generalize to the larger 3B scale.
All models share the same training configuration, except for the design choices varied in each intervention.
We use the Apertus architecture \citep{apertus_apertus_2025}, developed for multilingual language modeling, as our default configuration and vary its design choices through controlled interventions. 
Models are trained on DCLM-edu and FineWeb-2 data with a token budget proportional to model size and a warmup-stable-decay learning-rate schedule.
Appendix~\ref{app:model-ladder} provides the complete training grid and hyperparameters.

\subsubsection{Intervention Axes}

We consider six pretraining design choices, spanning the number and selection of training languages, data sampling temperature, model depth, optimizer, and activation function.

\textbf{Number of training languages.} We train models on $1, 2, 8, 15, 30, 50$ languages. The monolingual setting uses only English, while the multilingual settings allocate half the training tokens to English and half to the other remaining languages.

\textbf{Language selection scheme.}
We compare two approaches for selecting the non-English training languages. Scheme A prioritizes languages with larger available corpora, whereas scheme B prioritizes diversity in scripts and language families.
% At $K=2$ the schemes vary the second language: Russian (A), Chinese (B), or Spanish (C).
% At $K=1$ they vary the English corpus: DCLM-edu (A), the same DCLM pipeline without its educational-quality filter (B), or FineWeb (C).
Appendix~\ref{app:data-mixtures} lists the languages selected by each scheme at every language count.

\textbf{Sampling temperature.}
We compare two sampling temperatures for allocating training tokens across non-English languages: \textit{proportional sampling}, which reflects the relative size of each language's available corpus, and \textit{flattened sampling}, which increases the representation of languages with smaller corpora.
% The $T=3$ runs use the scheme-A lists at $K \in \{15, 30, 50\}$, with both architectures at $K=50$ and the deep architecture only at $K=15$ and $K=30$.

\textbf{Depth.} We compare a deep with a shallow-wide architecture at each size up to 1.7B. The shallow models have approximately twice the width-to-depth ratio while maintaining the same feed-forward multiplier and ratio of query to key-value attention heads. 
% Their non-embedding counts differ from the corresponding deep models by 0.4\% to 5.2\%. 

\textbf{Optimizer.}
We use AdEMAMix by default \citep{pagliardini_ademamix_2024}. For models trained on $8$, $15$, or $30$ languages, we also evaluate Muon \citep{jordan_muon_2024}, following the Moonlight recipe \citep{liu_muon_2025}.

\textbf{Activation.}

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{subsec:evaluation}

We evaluate all models at aligned checkpoints throughout training to compare their rankings across model sizes and training progress.
Specifically, we evaluate models at every 10\% of their training budget, from 10\% to 100\%, with two additional checkpoints at 85\% and 95\% used to estimate checkpoint noise. 
At each checkpoint, we measure model performance using held-out text likelihood and downstream benchmarks. 
Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation} provides the complete evaluation setup and implementation details.

\textbf{Evaluation suite.}
We evaluate models using held-out text bits-per-byte (BPB) and \nbenchanalysed downstream benchmarks covering 50 languages and a range of capabilities, including reading comprehension, commonsense reasoning, factual knowledge, and grammatical acceptability (Table~\ref{tab:app_benchmarks_considered}).  
BPB measures the negative log-likelihood of held-out text normalized by its UTF-8 byte count, with lower values indicating better performance. 
We include BPB as a reference to established loss-based scaling behavior and a point of comparison for downstream benchmarks. 
By default, each model is evaluated only on languages included in its training mixture.

\textbf{Answer formats \& scoring protocols.}
The downstream benchmarks include three answer formats: multiple-choice letters, cloze, and generative continuation. 
We evaluate these tasks using length-normalized accuracy and benchmark bits-per-byte (bBPB). 
Accuracy measures whether the model selects the correct answer, while bBPB measures the negative log-likelihood of the gold answer normalized by its UTF-8 byte length, with lower values indicating better performance. 
Unlike accuracy, bBPB does not have a chance-level baseline, allowing us to evaluate benchmarks even when model accuracy is near chance.

\textbf{Above-chance filtering.}
To avoid interpreting near-random accuracy as meaningful performance, we retain only benchmark tasks that demonstrate above-chance performance. 
An evaluation is considered above chance when the lower bound of its one-sided 95\% Wilson confidence interval exceeds the task's chance level. 
We retain a benchmark–language–model-size combination when at least half of its final-checkpoint evaluations satisfy this condition. This criterion accounts for differences in the number of evaluation examples across tasks. BPB and bBPB are exempt from this filtering because they have no defined chance level. 
Appendix~\ref{app:chance-threshold} provides further details on the filtering procedure.

\subsection{Ranking Agreement}
\label{subsec:methodology-da}

Ranking agreement (RA) measures whether a set of model variants is ranked in the same order at a cheaper proxy point and at the reference point. 
A proxy point corresponds to a smaller model size and or an earlier training checkpoint, while the reference point is the final checkpoint at the same model size or at the largest size shared by the compared variants.
We quantify ranking agreement using three complementary metrics. Spearman's rank correlation $\rho$ measures agreement between the complete proxy and reference rankings.
Kendall's $\tau$ measures the difference between the concordant and discordant pairs of variants.
Decision accuracy (DA; \citealp{heineman_signal_2025}) measures the fraction of pairwise preferences that agree between the proxy and reference points.
We report DA because the three measurements correlate at $r=0.97-0.99$ (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_agreement_correlation}).
We report DA only for comparisons involving at least three pairs of variants, since comparing only two variants yields a binary ranking agreement (0 or 1).
For analyses of ranking agreement, we retain tasks with a median DA of at least 0.66 across proxy points in either model-size or training-checkpoint comparisons. This heuristic threshold selects tasks with higher observed ranking agreement.
We consider three transfer settings defined in Table~\ref{tab:ra_defs}, with formal definitions privided in Appendix~\ref{app:ranking-agreement}.
