\section{Model Ladder Details}
\label{app:methodology}

This appendix describes the runs we train. \S\ref{app:training-grid} gives the training grid, the seeds and the checkpoint schedule. \S\ref{app:model-configurations} gives the model shapes and the optimization settings of each ladder. The data mixtures and the evaluation protocol follow in Appendices~\ref{app:data-mixtures} and~\ref{app:evaluation-protocol}.


\subsection{Training Grid and Checkpoints}
\label{app:training-grid}

\textbf{Grid.} A run is defined by its size, its number of training languages $K$, its ladder, its data build and its seed. A ladder fixes the model recipe (architecture, activation and optimizer) and a data build fixes the training data (Appendix~\ref{app:data-mixtures}). Table~\ref{tab:schemes} lists every combination of data build and ladder that we train, with its language settings, its sizes and its number of runs. The deep ladder on data build A is the baseline recipe.

\textbf{Seeds.} Every configuration trains with seed 1904. Ten deep configurations also train two replicate seeds each, 20 runs in total. The replicates measure how far a ranking can move for reasons unrelated to the design choice, since each seed changes both the initialization and the data order. Table~\ref{tab:grid} shows where they are. Seeds 64 and 313 train at 175M and 600M for $K \in \{1, 2, 50\}$. Seeds 28 and 1797 train at 1B for $K \in \{1, 2, 30\}$ on data build A, and at $K=30$ on data build B. The replicates cover only 175M, 600M and 1B, so they do not measure seed noise at 1.7B directly. Every other ladder and data build (shallow, SwiGLU, Muon, AT3, ZH, ES, DCLMP and FWEB) trains seed 1904 alone. These runs are levels of an intervention and do not enter the seed-noise estimate.

\textbf{Checkpoints.} Each run saves 20 evenly spaced checkpoints. The larger rungs save more, 40 at 1B and 60 at 1.7B and 3B. Since 40 and 60 are multiples of 20, every run has a checkpoint at each fraction $k/20$ of training, for $k=1,\ldots,20$. We evaluate twelve of them per run: the ten tenths of training and the saves at 85\% and 95\%. The analysis reads the ten tenths. Because every size shares them, we can compare sizes at the same multiple of the Chinchilla budget: 20\% of a run is 1C and its end is 5C. The two extra saves serve the checkpoint-noise estimate. With them, the last 20\% of training, which is the decay phase of the learning-rate schedule, holds five evenly spaced checkpoints (80, 85, 90, 95 and 100\%), and we estimate checkpoint-to-checkpoint noise over these five.\footnote{Sixteen 1B runs were trained before we raised the 1B rung to 40 checkpoints. They save 20 checkpoints, every 2,287 steps up to step 45,740, instead of every 1,143 steps up to step 45,720. Their saves land on the same $k/20$ fractions, so they are evaluated at the same points as every other run. The token count at matched checkpoints differs by 0.044\%.}

\textbf{Compute axis.} Every checkpoint has a known size $N$ and token count $D$, so results can also be read against training compute. We estimate $\mathrm{FLOPs} = 6\,(N + d_{\mathrm{model}} V)\,D$, where $V$ is the vocabulary size. The estimate leaves out the embedding lookup but counts the output projection, which is large here: with the 131K vocabulary, it roughly doubles the per-token cost of the smallest rungs.

\subsection{Model Configurations}
\label{app:model-configurations}

\textbf{Parameter-count convention.} All sizes count non-embedding parameters, that is, the transformer blocks without the tied token embedding and output projection. This is the ladder convention of \citet{bhagia_establishing_2025}, which \citet{heineman_signal_2025} also adopt. It matters here because the vocabulary is large. With 131,072 tokens, the tied embedding matrix alone has $d_{\mathrm{model}} \times V$ parameters, 101M at the 90M rung and 302M at the 1.7B rung. Counting it would compress the smallest models together on the size axis.

\textbf{Architecture.} All models are decoder-only transformers that follow the Apertus pretraining recipe \citep{apertus_apertus_2025}. They use grouped-query attention \citep{ainslie_gqa_2023} with four query heads per key-value head and a head dimension of 64, rotary position embeddings \citep{su_roformer_2023} with base 500,000 and Llama-3-style scaling factor 8, RMSNorm, QK-layernorm and the xIELU activation \citep{huang_xielu_2025}. They have no bias terms and no dropout. The sequence length is 4,096. Input and output embeddings are tied, over the 131,072-token Apertus tokenizer (\texttt{swiss-ai/Apertus-70B-2509}). Table~\ref{tab:ladder} gives the shape of every rung.

\textbf{Deep ladder.} The baseline ladder grows depth, width and the number of attention heads together. From 175M to 1B it keeps a width-to-depth ratio $d_{\mathrm{model}}/n_{\mathrm{layers}}$ of 64, and at every size a feed-forward multiplier of 4. The ratio is 51 at 90M, 77 at 1.7B and 78 at 3B, because these rungs need other shapes to reach their target sizes.

\textbf{Shallow ladder.} The depth intervention trains a second ladder at the same six sizes from 90M to 1.7B, with roughly twice the width-to-depth ratio (110--146 against 51--77). To keep the two ladders comparable, the shallow shapes keep the deep feed-forward multiplier and the deep ratio of query heads to key-value heads. We restrict $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ to multiples of 256 and, among the shapes within 4\% of the target size, pick the one whose ratio is closest to 128. The shallow sizes land within 0.4\% to 5.2\% of the deep ones, and each shallow run trains on its own budget $D = 100N$.

\textbf{SwiGLU ladder.} The activation intervention trains the deep ladder with SwiGLU in place of xIELU, on data build A at $K \in \{8, 15, 30\}$ and from 90M to 1.7B. It keeps every deep dimension except the feed-forward width. SwiGLU has three feed-forward projections instead of two, so the width shrinks to about $2.67\,d_{\mathrm{model}}$, which keeps the non-embedding count within 0.6\% of the deep one. Each run again trains on its own budget $D = 100N$.

\textbf{Optimization.} All runs share one learning-rate schedule, and all runs outside the Muon ladder share one optimizer. Only the per-size values change. We train with AdEMAMix \citep{pagliardini_ademamix_2024} ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\beta_3 = 0.9999$, $\alpha = 8$), weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at 0.1. The warmup of the AdEMAMix $\alpha$ and $\beta_3$ spans the full run, so it scales with run length. From 350M up, the global batch is 504 sequences of 4,096 tokens, about 2.06M tokens per step. The 90M and 175M rungs train at 84 and 168 sequences, for the reason given below. The learning rate follows a warmup-stable-decay schedule \citep{hagele_scaling_2024}: a linear warmup over about 4\% of the steps, a constant phase, and a $1-\sqrt{\cdot}$ decay to zero over the final 20\%. The peak learning rate comes from a compute-based law evaluated at the budget of each run, $\eta = 0.3118\, C^{-1/8}$ with $C = 6 N D = 600 N^2$. It decreases smoothly from $1.43 \times 10^{-3}$ at 90M to $6.9 \times 10^{-4}$ at 1.7B and $6.0 \times 10^{-4}$ at 3B. The initialization standard deviation scales with width as $0.008944 \sqrt{1792 / d_{\mathrm{model}}}$. Training runs in a fork of Megatron-LM \citep{shoeybi_megatron-lm_2019}, with BF16 mixed precision, FP32 gradient accumulation and data parallelism only, without tensor or pipeline parallelism.

\textbf{Batch size.} The 90M and 175M rungs train at a smaller global batch than the rest of the ladder, because at the shared 504-sequence batch the 90M runs diverge. Nine of the ten completed 90M runs at that batch reach their best training loss between 15\% and 19\% of the way through training and then degrade for the rest of the run, ending 1.2--1.9 nats above their own best. No larger rung shows this. Held-out bits-per-byte also rises with training on every language we checked, so the model itself degrades, not only its training loss. Retraining the rung at three learning rates that span a factor of five, or at one half and one sixth of the batch, reproduces the failure at the same fraction of the optimizer steps. This points to an interaction between the fixed $\beta_3$ timescale of AdEMAMix, about 10,000 steps, and a run that is only 4,500 steps long. The 175M run is 8,540 steps long, and its batch-504 runs also land off the power law of the larger rungs. We therefore train these two rungs at their own global batch, 84 sequences at 90M and 168 at 175M (the nearest value that the 175M data-parallel layout allows), with $D = 100N$ fixed. The step count grows by the same factor, to 27,000 and 25,620 steps, so the $\beta_3$ memory covers about 0.37 and 0.39 of the run. This removes the divergence without changing any optimizer constant, which the step-matched batch cuts above did not achieve. No run of the grid diverges, and the batch-504 runs of these two rungs enter no analysis.

\textbf{Muon ladder.} The optimizer intervention trains the deep ladder with Muon \citep{jordan_muon_2024} on data build A at $K \in \{8, 15, 30\}$ and from 90M to 1.7B, with every model dimension unchanged. We follow the Moonlight recipe \citep{liu_muon_2025}. Muon trains the two-dimensional hidden weight matrices with momentum 0.95 and Nesterov updates, and it orthogonalizes each update with five Newton-Schulz steps. It then rescales each update by $0.2\sqrt{\max(m, n)}$ for an $m \times n$ matrix, which matches the root mean square of an AdamW update. Muon can therefore reuse the deep ladder's peak learning rate, schedule and weight decay of 0.1. Embeddings, the output layer, norms and biases are trained with AdamW ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$). Muon keeps one momentum buffer per matrix, and in our fork its optimizer state is not sharded across data-parallel ranks.
