\section{Model Ladder}
\label{app:model-ladder}
\label{app:models}

We construct a controlled multilingual ladder of \nmodels models to measure ranking agreement across model size, training progress, and training design interventions. 
%\textbf{Number of parameters $N$.}
We train seven model sizes, measured in non-embedding parameters: 90M, 175M, 350M, 600M, 1B, 1.7B, 3B. We use the models from 90M to 1B parameters to predict performance at the 1.7B scale, and use the 3B size as validation.
All runs share the same training recipe described below, except for the factor varied by each intervention. 
This appendix gives the shared configuration (\S\ref{app:model-configurations}), the intervention axes (\S\ref{app:intervention-axes}) and the training grid (\S\ref{app:training-grid}). Appendices~\ref{app:data-mixtures} and~\ref{app:evaluation-protocol} describe the data mixtures and the evaluation protocol.

\subsection{Shared Configuration}
\label{app:model-configurations}

\textbf{Training budget.}
We define model size $N$ as the number of transformer-block parameters, excluding the tied embedding matrix. Each model receives a token budget proportional to its exact non-embedding parameter count:
$D(N) = 100N.$
This corresponds to five times the $20N$ Chinchilla token-budget convention, %\citep{hoffmann_training_2022},
following the fixed-multiple ladder design of 
%\citet{bhagia_establishing_2024} and 
\citet{magnusson_datadecide_2025}.

\textbf{Parameter-count convention.} All sizes are non-embedding parameters, that is, the transformer blocks alone, without the tied token embedding and output projection. This follows the ladder convention of \citet{bhagia_establishing_2025}, which \citet{heineman_signal_2025} also adopt. The convention matters here because of the 131,072-token vocabulary. The tied embedding matrix alone has $d_{\mathrm{model}} \times V$ parameters, 101M at the 90M rung and 302M at the 1.7B rung. Counting it would substantially change the relative spacing of the smallest models on the size axis.

\textbf{Training corpora.}
We draw English data from DCLM-edu \citep{li_datacomp-lm_2025} and data for the remaining languages from the quality-filtered FineWeb-2 collection \citep{penedo_fineweb2_2025,messmer_enhancing_2026}. % We tokenize all corpora with the Apertus tokenizer.

\textbf{Architecture.} All models are decoder-only transformers that follow the Apertus pretraining recipe \citep{apertus_apertus_2025}. They use grouped-query attention \citep{ainslie_gqa_2023} with four query heads per key-value head and a head dimension of 64. They use rotary position embeddings \citep{su_roformer_2023} with base 500,000 and Llama-3-style scaling factor 8, RMSNorm, and QK-layernorm. They have no bias terms and no dropout. The sequence length is 4,096. Input and output embeddings are tied, over the 131,072-token Apertus tokenizer (\texttt{swiss-ai/Apertus-70B-2509}).

\textbf{Optimization.} Every run uses the same optimizer and schedule. Only the per-size values change. We train by default with \textbf{AdEMAMix} \citep{pagliardini_ademamix_2024} ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$, $\beta_3 = 0.9999$, $\alpha = 8$), weight decay 0.1 and gradient clipping at 0.1. From 350M up, the global batch is 504 sequences of 4,096 tokens, about 2.06M tokens per step. The 90M and 175M rungs train at 84 and 168 sequences (see Batch size paragraph below). The learning rate follows a warmup-stable-decay schedule \citep{hagele_scaling_2024}. It has a linear warmup over about 4\% of the steps, a constant phase, and a $1-\sqrt{\cdot}$ decay to zero over the final 20\%. The peak learning rate comes from a compute-based law evaluated at the budget of each run, $\eta = 0.3118\, C^{-1/8}$ with $C = 6 N D = 600 N^2$. It therefore decreases smoothly from $1.43 \times 10^{-3}$ at 90M to $6.9 \times 10^{-4}$ at 1.7B and $6.0 \times 10^{-4}$ at 3B. We scale the optimizer warmup with run length and the initialization with width. The warmup of the AdEMAMix $\alpha$ and $\beta_3$ spans the full run. The initialization standard deviation is scaled with width as $0.008944 \sqrt{1792 / d_{\mathrm{model}}}$. Training uses BF16 mixed precision with FP32 gradient accumulation in a fork of Megatron-LM \citep{shoeybi_megatron-lm_2019}. It uses data parallelism only (no tensor or pipeline parallelism).

\textbf{Batch size.} At the 504-sequence batch, nine of the ten completed 90M runs diverge. Each of them reaches its best training loss between 15\% and 19\% of the way through training and then degrades for the rest of the run. It ends 1.2--1.9 nats above its own best. No larger rung shows this. Held-out bits-per-byte rises with training on every language we checked, so the degradation is real and not an artefact of the training objective. We retrained the rung at three learning rates that span a factor of five, and at one half and one sixth of the batch size. Each retraining reproduces the failure at the same fraction of the optimizer steps. This points to the interaction between the fixed $\beta_3$ timescale of AdEMAMix, about 10,000 steps, and a run that is only 4,500 steps long. The 175M run is 8,540 steps long, and its batch-504 runs also land off the power law of the larger rungs. We therefore train these two rungs at their own global batch, 84 sequences at 90M and 168 at 175M (the nearest value that the 175M data-parallel layout allows), with $D = 100N$ fixed. The step count grows by the same factor, to 27,000 and 25,620 steps, so the $\beta_3$ memory covers about 0.37 and 0.39 of the run. This removes the divergence without changing any optimizer constant, which the step-matched batch cuts above did not do. No run of the grid diverges, and the batch-504 runs of these two rungs enter no analysis.

\subsection{Intervention Axes}
\label{app:intervention-axes}

\textbf{Number of training languages ($K$).} We vary over $K \in \{1, 2, 8, 15, 30, 50\}$. The $K=1$ setting is English only. Multilingual settings allocate half the training tokens to English and half to the other $K-1$ languages.

\textbf{Language selection scheme ($M$).}
We compare two rules for choosing the non-English languages. Scheme A ranks languages by available corpus size, whereas scheme B prioritizes diversity in script and language family.
At $K=2$ the schemes vary the second language: Russian (A), Chinese (B), or Spanish (C). At $K=1$ they vary the English corpus: DCLM-edu (A), the same DCLM pipeline without its educational-quality filter (B), or FineWeb (C).
Appendix~\ref{app:data-mixtures} lists the languages selected by each scheme at every value of $K$.

\textbf{Sampling temperature ($T$).}
Within the non-English half of the training mixture, language $i$ has a sampling probability proportional to
$q_i \propto p_i^{1/T}$,
where $p_i$ is the language's estimated proportion of the available corpus. We use $T=1$ for proportional sampling and $T=3$ for flattened sampling.
% The $T=3$ runs use the scheme-A lists at $K \in \{15, 30, 50\}$, with both architectures at $K=50$ and the deep architecture only at $K=15$ and $K=30$.

\textbf{Architecture ($A$).} We compare a deep with a shallow-wide architecture at each size up to 1.7B. The shallow models roughly double the width-to-depth ratio while preserving the feed-forward multiplier and the ratio of query heads to key-value heads. % Their non-embedding counts differ from the corresponding deep models by 0.4\% to 5.2\%.

\textbf{Deep ladder.} The baseline ladder scales by increasing depth, width and the number of attention heads together. It keeps a width-to-depth ratio $d_{\mathrm{model}}/n_{\mathrm{layers}}$ of 64 from 175M to 1B and a feed-forward multiplier of 4. The ratio is 51 at 90M, 77 at 1.7B and 78 at 3B. These endpoint values are the compromises needed to hit the target sizes. It uses the xIELU activation \citep{huang_xielu_2025}. Table~\ref{tab:ladder} gives the configurations.

\textbf{Shallow ladder.} The depth intervention trains a second ladder at the same six non-embedding sizes, with roughly twice the width-to-depth ratio (110--146 against 51--77). To constrain the architecture comparison, the shallow search fixes the feed-forward multiplier and the ratio of query heads to key-value heads to the deep values. It restricts $d_{\mathrm{model}}$ to multiples of 256. Among the candidates within 4\% of the target size, it picks the ratio closest to 128. The shallow sizes land within 0.4\%--5.2\% of the deep ones, and each shallow run trains its own $D = 100N$.

\textbf{Optimizer ($O$).}
We use AdEMAMix by default \citep{pagliardini_ademamix_2024}. At $K \in \{8, 15, 30\}$, we include as alternative the optimizer Muon \citep{jordan_muon_2024}, following the Moonlight recipe \citep{liu_muon_2025}. % Muon updates the hidden weight matrices, and each update is rescaled by $0.2\sqrt{\max(m, n)}$ for an $m \times n$ matrix so that its size matches that of AdamW. This lets Muon reuse the same peak learning rate and weight decay. Embeddings, the output layer, norms and biases keep AdamW.

The optimizer intervention trains the deep ladder with \textbf{Muon} \citep{jordan_muon_2024} under scheme A at $K \in \{8, 15, 30\}$ and from 90M to 1.7B, with every model dimension unchanged. We follow the Moonlight recipe \citep{liu_muon_2025}. Muon applies to the two-dimensional hidden weight matrices, with momentum 0.95 and Nesterov updates, and orthogonalizes each update with five Newton-Schulz steps. Each update is then rescaled by $0.2\sqrt{\max(m, n)}$ for an $m \times n$ matrix, which matches the root mean square of an AdamW update. Muon therefore reuses the deep ladder's peak learning rate, schedule and weight decay of 0.1. Embeddings, the output layer, norms and biases are trained with AdamW ($\beta_1 = 0.9$, $\beta_2 = 0.999$). Muon keeps one momentum buffer per matrix, and in our fork its optimizer state is not sharded across data-parallel ranks.

\textbf{Activation ($A_f$).}
xIELU is the default activation function \citep{huang_xielu_2025}.
At $K \in \{8, 15, 30\}$, we also train the deep architecture with SwiGLU activation \citeneeded{}. %, with the feed-forward width reduced to keep the parameter count.

\textbf{SwiGLU ladder.} The activation intervention trains the deep ladder with SwiGLU in place of xIELU, on data build A at $K \in \{8, 15, 30\}$ and from 90M to 1.7B. It keeps every deep dimension except the feed-forward width. SwiGLU has three feed-forward projections instead of two, so the width shrinks to about $2.67\,d_{\mathrm{model}}$, which keeps the non-embedding count within 0.6\% of the deep one. Each run again trains on its own budget $D = 100N$.

\textbf{Checkpoints.} Each run saves evenly spaced checkpoints. There are 20 per run, 40 at the 1B rung and 60 at the 1.7B and 3B rungs, so the reference rungs are sampled more densely. Because 40 and 60 are multiples of 20, the denser grids contain the shared fractions $k/20$, for $k=1,\ldots,20$. The $20N$ Chinchilla token budget falls at one fifth of training. We evaluate twelve checkpoints per run. Ten of them are the tenths of training. Every size shares them, so we can compare sizes at the same multiple of Chinchilla (20\% of a run is 1C and its end is 5C). The other two are the 85\% and 95\% saves. The analysis reads the ten tenths. The two extra points let us estimate checkpoint-to-checkpoint noise over five evenly spaced checkpoints in the last 20\% of training (80, 85, 90, 95 and 100\%). This is the decay phase of the schedule.\footnote{Fifteen 1B cells were trained before the 40-checkpoint rule and save 20 checkpoints (every 2,287 steps to step 45,740, instead of every 1,143 steps to 45,720). Their saves land on the same $k/20$ fractions, so they are evaluated at the same points as every other run. The token count at matched checkpoints differs by 0.044\%.}

\textbf{Compute axis.} Every checkpoint has a known $(N, D)$, so results can also be read against training compute. We estimate $\mathrm{FLOPs} = 6\,(N + d_{\mathrm{model}} V)\,D$, where $V$ is the vocabulary size. We omit the embedding-lookup cost but include the output projection. With a 131K vocabulary, the projection nearly doubles the per-token cost of the smallest rungs.
