\section{Evaluation}
\label{app:evaluation}
\label{app:evaluation-protocol}

We evaluate every model on downstream benchmarks and on bits-per-byte (BPB) of held-out text. We evaluate both at the same aligned training fractions for every model size, so that rankings can be compared across size and training progress: the ten deciles from 10\% to 100\% of training and two more checkpoints at 85\% and 95\%. Every analysis along a run reads the ten deciles, which we label in Chinchilla multiples. Each run trains five times the $20N$ budget, so 20\% of a run is 1C and its end 5C. The 85\% and 95\% checkpoints serve the checkpoint-noise estimate, over five evenly spaced checkpoints in the last 20\% of training. Table~\ref{tab:notation-evals} gives the notation.

\subsection{Benchmarks and Tasks}
\label{app:benchmarks}

\textbf{Benchmark selection.} We considered \nbenchconsidered benchmarks, with \ntasksconsidered language tasks on the trained languages (Table~\ref{tab:app_benchmarks_considered}). We evaluate \nbenchanalysed of them throughout training and analyse them (\ntasksanalysed tasks). They span reading comprehension, commonsense, factual and cultural knowledge, reasoning, grammatical acceptability and other capabilities. We evaluated the other benchmarks only on the final checkpoints of the 600M, 1B, 1.7B and 3B models, and discarded the \ntasksdiscarded tasks that stayed at chance at both 1B and 1.7B. Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_chance_shared} gives the number of trained languages in which each benchmark is above chance.

\textbf{Task selection.} A model is evaluated on the registered tasks whose language is in its training mixture. A family can have several tasks per language, so there can be more tasks than families. For the scheme-A lists, the number of tasks grows with $K$: \ntasksKone at $K=1$, \ntasksKtwo at $K=2$, \ntasksKeight at $K=8$, \ntasksKfifteen at $K=15$, \ntasksKthirty at $K=30$, and \ntasksKfifty at $K=50$. These counts cover every group of Table~\ref{tab:app_benchmarks_considered}: the multilingual families, the language-specific benchmarks, and the reformulated variants of the letter-format families (\S\ref{subsec:rq1_factors}). Some runs are also evaluated on every task in every language, regardless of their training mixture. These are the deep seed-1904 runs of data build A at every $K$, of ZH and ES at $K=2$ and of B at $K=8$. They let us measure cross-lingual transfer on the benchmarks as well as on bits-per-byte.

\textbf{Language coverage.} Every language of the $K=50$ list is covered by at least one benchmark family, but coverage falls steeply towards the low-resource end of the list (Table~\ref{tab:app-languages}). English is covered by 31 families: 17 that also cover other languages and 14 English-only benchmarks. INCLUDE v2 (English) is not among them, although its text is English, because we file each of its exams under the exam's language. The other 49 languages have a median of eight families. The most covered are Spanish (18), Mandarin Chinese (17), Russian and Standard Arabic (14 each), and Hindi, Italian and Vietnamese (13 each). The least covered are mostly languages that enter only at $K=50$: Bosnian has Global PIQA alone, Standard Latvian has Belebele and the extra MultiBLiMP languages, and Slovenian has three families (Belebele, Global PIQA and MultiBLiMP). Thai, which enters at $K=30$, has four. For these languages, every benchmark result rests on one to four benchmarks.

\subsection{Answer Formats and Scoring}
\label{app:scoring}

\textbf{Answer formats.} A benchmark task poses its items in one of three formats. A \emph{letter} task lists the options in the prompt with labels (A, B, \ldots) and scores the label. A \emph{cloze} task scores each answer text as a continuation of the prompt. A \emph{completion} task has no shared prompt (minimal pairs, sentence pairs, Winograd schemas) or a single target continuation (LAMBADA). For the letter-format benchmarks we also evaluate reformulated versions of the same items in cloze format (RF and LLM-RF, \S\ref{sec:rq1}).

\textbf{Scoring.} Tasks whose options are answer strings of different lengths use length-normalised accuracy (\texttt{acc\_norm}): the cloze-format originals (ARC, HellaSwag, OpenBookQA, MathQA and Global PIQA), INCLUDE v2 and the reformulated versions. The other tasks use accuracy (\texttt{acc}), the harness default. On letter-format tasks the two are identical, since every option is a single label of the same length. A few cloze-style benchmarks keep the default accuracy, among them XNLI, XCOPA and XStoryCloze. Generative tasks use exact match. We aggregate sub-tasks, for example the MMLU subjects, to their family with the harness's own grouping.

\textbf{Harness settings.} All benchmarks run through a fork of the LM Evaluation Harness \citep{gao_framework_2021}. We install it from a pinned shared checkout, and each evaluation job records the source commit. We use the vLLM backend \citep{kwon_efficient_2023}, a maximum context of 4,096 tokens, a beginning-of-sequence token before every prompt, no chat template, and the default prompt and number of in-context examples of each task. Base models at these sizes are at chance on several families, so the above-chance threshold of Appendix~\ref{app:chance-threshold} decides, per task and size, whether a result enters the analysis.

\textbf{Above-chance threshold.} An evaluation run is above chance when the lower bound of its one-sided 95\% Wilson confidence interval exceeds the chance level of the task. We retain a (benchmark, language, size) cell when at least half of its runs are above chance. This prevents near-random variation from being read as evidence for or against scaling. Because the bound depends on the number of items, the threshold adapts to the size of each task instead of using a fixed margin. Measurements without a chance level, such as BPB and bBPB, are not filtered. Appendix~\ref{app:chance-threshold} gives the implementation and statistics of the threshold.

\textbf{Bits-per-byte.} Bits-per-byte (BPB) is the negative log-likelihood of held-out text in bits, divided by the UTF-8 byte count of the text. Lower is better. For language $\ell$, let $x_1,\ldots,x_{m_\ell}$ be the evaluated token sequence, $c_t$ the context preceding token $x_t$ within its scoring block, and $b_\ell$ the number of bytes in the evaluated text. We compute
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BPB}_\ell = \frac{-\sum_{t=2}^{m_\ell}\log_2 p(x_t \mid c_t)}{b_\ell}.
\label{eq:bpb}
\end{equation}
The evaluated text comes from the shared validation set of Appendix~\ref{app:data-mixtures}. We concatenate its documents for language $\ell$ without end-of-document separators and keep a deterministic prefix of this stream, which ends at the first document boundary once 1M tokens are reached, or the full stream if it is shorter. We score the prefix in blocks of 4,096 input tokens, with a one-token overlap between successive blocks, so the context resets at every block boundary. Each token except the first of the stream is scored once. The denominator $b_\ell$ is the UTF-8 byte count of the whole decoded prefix, including the first token, which only serves as context. The implementation thus omits the likelihood of the first token but keeps its bytes. We also compute token-level perplexity from the same forward passes.

We include BPB as a reference signal: it connects our analysis to loss-based scaling laws and gives a point of comparison for the scaling of the downstream benchmarks.

\textbf{Benchmark BPB.} Benchmark bits-per-byte (bBPB) scores a benchmark by the likelihood of its gold answers instead of by accuracy. It uses the per-item log-likelihoods that the harness records for the accuracy evaluation, so it needs no extra forward pass. For an item $x$ with gold answer $a^{*}$, the harness gives the log-likelihood $\log p(a^{*} \mid x)$ of the gold continuation in nats. Over the $n$ items of a task,
\[
\mathrm{bBPB}_{b_\ell} = \frac{1}{n} \sum_{x} \frac{-\log p(a^{*} \mid x)}{\ln 2 \cdot |a^{*}|},
\]
where $|a^{*}|$ is the length of the gold continuation in UTF-8 bytes. This is the \texttt{correct\_bpb} measure of OLMES \citeneeded{}, used by \citet{heineman_signal_2025}. Tasks whose target is not an option index have no bBPB: XWinograd, whose options change the context rather than the continuation, and LAMBADA, which has a single continuation. On a letter-format task the gold continuation is the option letter, so its bBPB measures how surprising the letter is, not the answer text. We mark the tasks whose mean gold length is at most 2.5 bytes as letter tasks and summarise them apart from the others. bBPB has no chance level, so the above-chance gate does not apply to it. We propose bBPB as a measurement for benchmarks whose accuracy is not above chance.
