\section{Above chance threshold}
\label{app:chance-threshold}

Small models are at chance on many benchmark tasks, and a score at chance says little about a design choice. We therefore test, for every language task and model size, whether the models score above chance, and analyse a task at a size only if it passes. This appendix defines the test (\S\ref{app:chance-threshold-definition}) and shows how strongly the answer format decides whether a task passes (\S\ref{app:chance-threshold-reformulation}).

\subsection{Threshold definition}
\label{app:chance-threshold-definition}

\textbf{Chance level.} For a task with $m_b$ answer options, chance accuracy is $1/m_b$. We read the option count from the evaluated samples where available, and from the benchmark's specification otherwise. When the option count varies across items, chance is the mean of $1/m$ over the items (0.2253 for TruthfulQA mc1). TruthfulQA mc2 scores probability mass instead of a single pick, so its chance level is the mean share of true options (0.449).

\textbf{Test per model.} For every model of size $N$, we take its final-checkpoint accuracy $\hat{p}$ over the $n$ scored items of the task. We recover the number of correct answers as $\operatorname{round}(\hat{p}\,n)$ and compute the one-sided 95\% Wilson lower confidence bound on the proportion. The model is above chance when
\[
\operatorname{LCB}_{95}(\hat{p}) > \frac{1}{m_b}.
\]

\textbf{Decision per task and size.} We pool these decisions over the models of the same size, that is, every ladder and every data build at seed 1904. A $(b_\ell, N)$ cell is retained if at least 50\% of these models are above chance. If some of these models train the language of the task, only they enter the decision. Otherwise, every evaluated model of that size does.

\textbf{Use in the analysis.} The resulting mask is the gate that every analysis reads. A gated cell keeps its place in every grid and table but carries no value. A scaling fit uses only the sizes where the task passes. A ranking against the reference also requires the task to pass at the reference size. The gate does not apply to measurements without a chance level, such as bits-per-byte, benchmark bits-per-byte and training loss.

\textbf{Per-benchmark threshold.} The Wilson bound depends on the number of items, so the margin by which accuracy must exceed chance differs across tasks. A small test set needs a large margin, while a large one can detect a small departure from chance. For example, the median required margin is 9.00 percentage points for nonparallel Global PIQA (100 items per language), 2.44 for Belebele (900), 0.74 for HellaSwag (about 9,000), and 0.60 for Global-MMLU (about 14,000). Figure~\ref{fig:app_chance_share} gives the share of language tasks that pass the threshold per benchmark.

\subsection{Task reformulation}
\label{app:chance-threshold-reformulation}

\textbf{The formulation effect.} The margin alone does not decide which benchmarks pass. Over the six sizes from 90M to 1.7B, the share of (benchmark, language, size) cells that pass the filter is 37.5\% for nonparallel Global PIQA, 1.1\% for Belebele, 100.0\% for HellaSwag and 4.1\% for Global-MMLU. Belebele and Global-MMLU need small margins, yet they fail almost everywhere. Both ask the model to answer with the letter of an option. Their reformulated variants pose the same items but score the answer strings instead of the letters (\S\ref{subsec:rq0}, Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-families}), and they pass in 74.9\% and 73.0\% of their cells. This suggests that the letter format is what keeps these benchmarks at chance.

Figure~\ref{fig:app_chance_reformulation} compares the share of language tasks that pass for each answer format, and Figure~\ref{fig:app_chance_full} gives the verdict for every language task.
