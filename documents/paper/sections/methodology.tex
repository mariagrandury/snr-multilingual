\subsection{Experimental Design}
\label{subsec:design}

We test whether design decisions made with small multilingual models transfer to larger models as the number of training languages increases. We describe the comparisons below, followed by the models (\S\ref{subsec:models}), pretraining data (\S\ref{subsec:data}), and evaluation (\S\ref{subsec:evaluation}). Appendix~\ref{app:methodology} gives the full configurations and implementation details.

\textbf{Scale and multilinguality.} We train six model sizes, measured in non-embedding parameters: 90M, 175M, 350M, 600M, 1B, and 1.7B. We vary the number of training languages over $L \in \{1, 2, 8, 15, 30, 50, 100\}$. The $L=1$ setting is English only; multilingual settings allocate half the training tokens to English and half to the other $L-1$ languages. Holding the English share fixed lets us study how distributing the remaining budget across more languages affects the reliability of small-scale decisions.

\textbf{Design decisions.} We compare four interventions: resource-ranked versus diversity-first language selection at $L \in \{8, 15, 30\}$; proportional versus flattened sampling at $L=50$; Russian, Chinese, or Spanish as the second language at $L=2$; and deep versus shallow-wide architectures. The $L=100$ setting uses flattened sampling only. Data interventions share the same model configuration, token budget, and tokenizer; the depth intervention approximately matches non-embedding size (\S\ref{subsec:models}). We measure decision accuracy on the languages or benchmark tasks shared by the compared levels. The reference is the largest available size shared by those levels: up to 1.7B, or 1B for comparisons involving Chinese or Spanish at $L=2$.

\textbf{Training budget.} We allocate tokens in proportion to each model's exact non-embedding parameter count $N$:
\begin{equation}
D(N) = 100N.
\label{eq:training-budget}
\end{equation}
This gives every model five times the $20N$ Chinchilla token-budget convention \citep{hoffmann_training_2022}, following the fixed-multiple ladder design of \citet{bhagia_establishing_2024} and \citet{magnusson_datadecide_2025}. We evaluate ten aligned fractions of training, including the final checkpoint, to compare decisions across both model size and training progress.

\textbf{Repeated runs.} We use seed 1904 throughout and three seeds for selected settings at 175M, 600M, and 1B. These runs vary both initialization and data order, so their variability estimates the combined effect of training randomness. We also measure checkpoint noise over the last five evaluated checkpoints. The full seed grid and checkpoint schedules are in Appendix~\ref{app:training-grid}.

\subsection{Pretrained Models}
\label{subsec:models}

\textbf{Shared architecture.} We train decoder-only transformers using the Apertus recipe \citep{apertus_apertus_2025}, with grouped-query attention \citep{ainslie_gqa_2023}, rotary position embeddings \citep{su_roformer_2023}, RMSNorm, QK-layernorm, and xIELU activations \citep{huang_xielu_2025}. All models use the same 131,072-token Apertus tokenizer, tied input and output embeddings, and a context length of 4,096 tokens. We count transformer-block parameters as model size, excluding the tied embedding matrix, following \citet{bhagia_establishing_2024} and \citet{heineman_signal_2025}. We include the output projection when estimating training compute, since its cost is substantial at small sizes.

\textbf{Depth intervention.} We compare a deep baseline with a shallow-wide architecture at each nominal size. The shallow models roughly double the width-to-depth ratio while preserving the feed-forward multiplier and the query-to-key-value head ratio. Their non-embedding counts differ from the deep models by 0.4\% to 5.2\%, and each receives its own $D=100N$ budget. This comparison therefore tests the architecture choice at approximately matched size; it does not hold parameter count, token budget, or projection cost exactly constant. Table~\ref{tab:ladder} gives both ladders.

\textbf{Optimization.} We use AdEMAMix \citep{pagliardini_ademamix_2024} and a warmup-stable-decay learning-rate schedule \citep{hagele_scaling_2024}, with a global batch of 504 sequences at every size. We scale the peak learning rate with the run's training budget and the initialization standard deviation with model width. This shared recipe limits differences in training procedure across the ladder; Appendix~\ref{app:model-configurations} specifies the hyperparameters.

\subsection{Pretraining Data}
\label{subsec:data}

\textbf{Corpora.} We draw English from DCLM-edu \citep{li_datacomp-lm_2025} and the remaining languages from the quality-filtered FineWeb-2 data used for Apertus \citep{penedo_fineweb2_2025, messmer_enhancing_2026, apertus_apertus_2025}. All corpora use the shared Apertus tokenizer.

\textbf{Language selection and sampling.} Scheme A selects languages by corpus size; scheme B prioritizes script and language-family diversity. Within the non-English half of each mixture, language $i$ receives a share proportional to $p_i^{1/T}$, where $p_i$ is its estimated corpus token proportion. We use $T=1$ for proportional sampling and $T=3$ for the flattened scheme AT3. Comparing both temperatures at $L=50$ helps distinguish the effect of sampling from that of adding languages at $L=100$. Appendix~\ref{app:languages} lists the languages, and Appendix~\ref{app:data-construction} documents selection and data repetition when a source is exhausted.

\textbf{Held-out data.} We construct one fixed validation set covering English and the 99 non-English languages. Training builds exclude the rows assigned to validation. We use the same validation text for a given language across model sizes, mixtures, and interventions, so within-language score differences reflect model differences on shared data.

\subsection{Evaluation}
\label{subsec:evaluation}

We use per-language bits-per-byte as the primary outcome and downstream benchmarks to test whether task scores support the same development decisions. Evaluating both throughout training lets us compare their ability to distinguish design choices from score variability.

\textbf{Bits-per-byte.} We measure the negative log-likelihood of held-out text in bits, normalized by its UTF-8 byte count. For language $\ell$, let $x_1,\ldots,x_{m_\ell}$ be its validation token prefix, $c_t$ the preceding context available within the scoring block, and $b_\ell$ the prefix's byte count:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{BPB}_\ell = \frac{-\sum_{t=2}^{m_\ell}\log_2 p(x_t \mid c_t)}{b_\ell}.
\label{eq:bpb}
\end{equation}
Lower BPB indicates better prediction of the held-out text. We score the same deterministic prefix of approximately 1M tokens per language for every model. Byte normalization avoids dependence on token counts, but does not make texts in different languages equally predictable. We therefore compare interventions within each language and give languages equal weight in macro averages. Such averages still depend on the language set; decision accuracy uses only languages shared by the compared levels.

\textbf{Downstream tasks.} We evaluate 14 benchmark families spanning reading comprehension, commonsense, knowledge, grammatical acceptability, and other capabilities (Table~\ref{tab:benchmark-families}). These include parallel multilingual items, native-language questions, machine translations, and automatically generated probes. Their varied construction and coverage let us examine where benchmark scores provide reliable decisions. We retain tasks with near-chance performance, since whether they distinguish small models is itself part of the study. Appendix~\ref{app:evaluation-protocol} gives the families, task counts, and scoring settings.

\textbf{Evaluation protocol.} We use the Swiss AI fork of the LM Evaluation Harness \citep{gao_framework_2024} with the vLLM backend \citep{kwon_efficient_2023}, each task's default prompt and number of in-context examples, and no chat template. By default, we evaluate BPB and downstream tasks for languages in the training mixture. For the seed-1904 deep scheme-A ladder, we additionally evaluate all validation languages and all registered benchmark languages, allowing us to study cross-lingual transfer to languages absent from training.
