\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern language model development increasingly relies on smaller proxy models and intermediate training checkpoints to guide decisions about data mixtures, architectures, tokenizers, and other training choices.
This practice relies on the assumption that smaller training runs are predictive of larger training runs. 
% For the results of these smaller training runs to guide training at scale, they must lead to making the same decisions as if the run had been performed with a fully trained larger model.
%If an evaluation allows us to make such decisions, 
This property of an evaluation configuration is its \emph{decision reliability}, \ie it results in the same decisions as if the ablations had been done on models of the target scale. Without this, small-scale ablations are not meaningful. %<maybe some brief argument of why determining this is important>. %and understanding the conditions under which
%A comparison read from a score at chance selects a candidate by noise, potentially leading to suboptimal decisions at the full cost of all these proxy runs.

Various aspects of decision reliability have been previously studied in the monolingual setting \citep{magnusson_datadecide_2025}, where empirically small proxy models provide good signal about which design choices are best at scale.
However, the multilingual language modelling setting introduces a host of new factors that could interact with the conditions under which decision reliability can be expected. 
Languages vary in training mixture allocation, tokenization cost, and scaling behaviour \citep{petrov_language_2023, ahia_all_2023,muenninghoff_data_constrained_2023}. Cross-lingual transfer and interference dynamics further couple decisions across languages, meaning that adding data for one language can help or hurt others depending on model capacity and language similarity \citep{chang-etal-2024-multilinguality, longpre_atlas_2026}. 
%The token budget of a proxy run is also divided among its languages: while a total token budget may look large, languages receiving small fractions of the share can easily be undertrained. 
Empirical evidence from prior work shows that decision reliability for the multilingual setting may require different conditions: many multilingual language--benchmark pairs are known to exhibit little measurable signal at small scales, with performance often remaining near chance or floor levels \citeneeded{}. 
Together, this suggests that decision reliability findings in monolingual settings cannot be transferred directly to multilingual settings, warranting their own investigation.
