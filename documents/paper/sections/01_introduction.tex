Modern language model development increasingly relies on smaller proxy models and intermediate training checkpoints to guide decisions about data mixtures, data sampling strategies, architectures, tokenizers, and other training choices. 
%and are typically evaluated with benchmark suites that differ substantially in their diagnostic value and variance. 
For the results of these smaller training runs to guide training at scale, they must lead to making the same decisions as if the run had been performed with a fully-trained larger model. This predictability has been previously studied in the monolingual setting \citep{magnusson_datadecide_2025}, but multilingual language model development introduces a host of other factors that could cause proxy model results to become noisy, such as cross-language transfer and interference, and an expansive space of possible data mixtures as the number of languages grows. 
% We see empirical evidence of this already: many multilingual language–benchmark are known to exhibit little measurable signal at small scales, with performance often remaining near chance or floor levels. 
% These factors make the language dimension a substantial expansion of the experimental space.

Multilingual scaling laws provide one approach for predicting the scaling behavior of LLMs, by modeling how performance changes with model size, data allocation, and language mixture, and by accounting for cross-language transfer \citep{he2025scaling}. 
However, scaling laws focus primarily on training loss and likelihood-based objectives, where scaling behavior is often smoother and measurable earlier in training. In contrast, many training ablations are run to predict the effect of model or data decision on more complex downstream behaviors (such as knowledge retention or commonsense reasoning), which is typically measured by performance on benchmarks. Consequently, providing useful insights for scaling behavior  requires informative benchmarks that can (a) robustly disentangle the performance effects of training decisions when evaluating small-scale models, and (b) encourage decisions at small scale that would have the same effect on larger-scale models.

Recent work has begun to examine the diagnostic value and efficiency of language model benchmarks, recognizing that not all benchmarks provide equally informative signals for guiding model development.
Benchmark-discrimination analyses such as SNR \citep{heineman_signal_2025}, MetaEval \citep{wang2026metaeval} and \citet{chen_scaling_2025, gupta_improving_2025, zhou_lost_2026} characterize whether an evaluation contains enough reliable signal, and have largely been developed in settings where evaluation signal is already measurable. However, it is unclear whether their conclusions transfer to multilingual model development, where downstream performance can remain near floor at small scales and ranking agreement may differ substantially across languages. 
Languages vary in data availability, tokenization cost, and scaling behavior \citep{petrov_language_2023, ahia_all_2023}, while cross-lingual transfer further couples decisions across languages, meaning that adding data for one language can help or hurt others depending on model capacity and language similarity \citep{chang_when_2023, longpre_atlas_2026}. As a result, a benchmark that provides a reliable training signal for one language or scale may be uninformative for another.

To study these questions, we pretrain a controlled multilingual model ladder that varies model size, model architecture and language coverage.
We evaluate models throughout training using per-language bits per byte (BPB) and \nbenchmarks multilingual benchmarks.
This setup allows us to (i) characterize the predictability of multilingual evaluations across model size and training, (ii) determine how early small-scale evaluations recover full-scale training decisions, and (iii) assess whether cheaper surrogate measurements can anticipate the reliability of those decisions.

This work makes the following main contributions:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=15pt,noitemsep,topsep=0pt]
    \item A controlled \textbf{multilingual scaling ladder} and open model release, spanning model sizes, training checkpoints, language mixtures, design interventions, and seed replicates.     \item A methodological \textbf{framework for characterizing multilingual evaluations} for model-development decisions, measuring how predictably benchmarks scale across model size and training progress, and how early they recover the decisions made at larger scale.
    \item An \textbf{analysis of decision reliability} and cross-language generalization, comparing cheap surrogates for ranking agreement across multilingual regimes and testing when scaling and decision patterns transfer across languages.
\end{itemize}
