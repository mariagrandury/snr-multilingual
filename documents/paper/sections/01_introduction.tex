\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Modern language model development increasingly relies on smaller proxy models and intermediate training checkpoints to guide decisions about data mixtures, sampling strategies, architectures, tokenizers, and other training choices. 
%and are typically evaluated with benchmark suites that differ substantially in their diagnostic value and variance. 
For these training runs and evaluations to be useful, their results must lead to the same decisions as ablations with a fully-trained larger model. 
In other words, results need to be predictive across either model sizes or stages of training. 
This has been studied in the monolingual setting \cite{magnusson_datadecide_2025}, but multilingual language model development introduces a host of other factors that could cause proxy model results to become noisy: cross-language transfer and interference, uneven benchmark coverage across languages, a rapidly expanding space of possible data-mixture configurations as the number of languages grows... 
We see empirical evidence of this already: many multilingual language–benchmark are known to exhibit little measurable signal at small scales, with performance often remaining near chance or floor levels. 
These factors make the language dimension a substantial expansion of the experimental space.


% why multilingual evaluation makes decision reliability harder.

Recent work has begun to examine the diagnostic value and efficiency of language model benchmarks, recognizing that not all benchmarks provide equally informative signals for guiding model development.
Benchmark-discrimination analyses such as SNR \citep{heineman_signal_2025}, MetaEval \citep{wang2026metaeval} and \citet{chen_scaling_2025, gupta_improving_2025, zhou_lost_2026} characterize whether an evaluation contains enough reliable signal, and have largely been developed in settings where evaluation signal is already measurable. It is unclear whether their conclusions transfer to multilingual model development, where downstream performance can remain near floor at small scales and ranking agreement may differ substantially across languages. 
Languages vary in data availability, tokenization cost, and scaling behavior \citep{petrov_language_2023, ahia_all_2023}, while cross-lingual transfer further couples decisions across languages, meaning that adding data for one language can help or hurt others depending on model capacity and language similarity \citep{chang_when_2023, longpre_atlas_2026}. As a result, a benchmark that provides a reliable training signal for one language or scale may be uninformative for another.

% why existing multilingual scaling work is not sufficient.

Multilingual scaling laws address a complementary part of this problem by modeling how performance changes with model size, data allocation, and language mixture, and by accounting for cross-language transfer \citep{he2025scaling}. 
However, existing work has focused primarily on training loss or likelihood-based objectives, where scaling behavior is often smoother and measurable earlier in training. 
It remains unclear whether the same conclusions hold for downstream multilingual evaluations, whose signals may emerge later and scale irregularly. This makes it important to understand when downstream benchmark performance becomes predictable, when the decisions it supports stabilize across scale, and whether cheap measurements can tell us when to trust them.



To study these questions, we pretrain a controlled multilingual model ladder that varies model size, model architecture and language coverage.
We evaluate models throughout training using per-language bits per byte (BPB) and \nbenchmarks multilingual benchmarks.
This setup allows us to (i) characterize the predictability of multilingual evaluations across model size and training, (ii) determine how early small-scale evaluations recover full-scale training decisions, and (iii) assess whether cheaper surrogate measurements can anticipate the reliability of those decisions.
