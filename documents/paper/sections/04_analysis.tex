\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We ask when small multilingual models provide useful evidence for larger-scale development. We first examine how scores change with model size (\S\ref{subsec:rq1}), then test whether design decisions transfer across size and training progress (\S\ref{subsec:rq2}). We next assess statistics that predict decision accuracy (\S\ref{subsec:rq3}), compare interventions with different effect sizes (\S\ref{subsec:rq4}), and test transfer across languages (\S\ref{subsec:rq5}).

\textbf{The ladder.} We analyze 98 model runs. Every cell is scored on all 100 validation languages. A design decision is read against the largest size trained, i.e., 1.7B.

\textbf{The above-random gate.} We retain a (benchmark, size) cell when its mean score exceeds chance by 0.05, with chance taken from the task's option count. This is a performance threshold, not a statistical significance test. Of the 462 tasks that have a chance level, 114 clear it at some size and 348 never do. The gate opens with size: 3 tasks clear it at 90M, 62 at 175M, 72 at 350M, 87 at 600M, 99 at 1B and 112 at 1.7B. BPB has no chance level and is never gated.

\subsection{RQ1: What scales predictably?}
\label{subsec:rq1}

\textbf{Finding.} Several completion-format benchmarks and per-language BPB follow a strong log-linear trend in model size, with median $R^2$ above 0.9. Knowledge benchmarks lik Global-MMLU, INCLUDE, and Belebele have median $R^2$ below 0.3.

\textbf{Setup.} For every task and language setting we fit the final-checkpoint score of the deep, scheme-A, seed-1904 ladder against $\log_{10} N$ over the rungs from 175M to 1.7B, and record the coefficient of determination $R^2$ and the Spearman correlation $\rho$ between score and size. A fit needs at least three rungs. Bits per byte and the training loss fall as models grow, so $\rho = -1$ is their ideal. We report medians over each family's (task, $L$) fits: 3,408 fits over 568 tasks, of which 2,802 are benchmark fits. The 90M rung is excluded (\S\ref{subsec:models}).

\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rq1_scaling.pdf}
\caption{Which benchmarks scale predictably. (a) Accuracy above chance against non-embedding parameters at $L=30$, one faint line per language task and a bold line per family median, for two families that scale (MultiBLiMP, HellaSwag), one that starts to (XNLI), and two that stay at chance up to 1.7B (Belebele, Global-MMLU). (b) Median $R^2$ of the log-linear fit and median Spearman $\rho$ against size per family, with the number of (task, $L$) fits and the answer count. Bits per byte and loss fall with size, so $\rho=-1$ is correct for them.}
\label{fig:rq1}

\textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:rq1} shows substantial differences across benchmark families. Multilingual LAMBADA ($R^2 = 0.99$), per-language bits per byte (0.95), XStoryCloze (0.94), XWinograd (0.94), the training loss (0.94) and HellaSwag (0.91) have strong log-linear fits, and MultiBLiMP (0.85, $\rho = 0.97$ over 342 fits) and XNLI (0.81) follow. At the other end, Global-MMLU ($R^2 = 0.22$, $\rho = -0.2$), INCLUDE (0.25, $\rho = 0.0$) and Belebele (0.29) have scores that are close to unrelated to size, and Global PIQA sits in between (0.34).

\textbf{Task format.} Fit quality also varies with answer count: the median $R^2$ is 0.64 over the 1,152 two-option fits and 0.81 over the 108 three-option ones, against 0.39 over the 1,512 four-option fits. Panel (a) shows why. At $L = 30$ the illustrated four-option families remain near chance up to 1.7B, so there is no trend to fit, while MultiBLiMP is 0.24 above chance already at 175M. TruthfulQA is the one family with a tight fit and a negative slope ($R^2 = 0.68$, $\rho = -0.57$): it reliably gets worse as models grow, an inverse-scaling pattern in this evaluation. A high $R^2$ therefore indicates a consistent trend, not necessarily improvement. Answer count alone also cannot explain the family differences: HellaSwag has four options and a strong fit. Task format, capability, and difficulty remain confounded in this comparison.

\subsection{RQ2: How early can we make the right decision?}
\label{subsec:rq2}

\textbf{Finding.} On per-language BPB, a 350M proxy matches the available reference's language-list ranking at 8, 15 and 30 languages. The reference is 1.7B at 8 and 30 languages and 1B at 15 languages. Its ranking already matches the final ranking at 20\% of training. Benchmark agreement remains close to one half across the evaluated sizes and checkpoints.

\textbf{Setup.} For an intervention with two levels, decision accuracy reduces to sign agreement: over a population of items, the share on which the proxy prefers the same level as the reference. The reference is the largest size trained at both levels, read at its final checkpoint. The proxy is every smaller size, read at the checkpoint nearest 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100\% of its own run, so that one grid answers both halves of the question, how small and how early. Two populations are used: the per-language bits per byte of the languages both levels train on, and the benchmark tasks both levels were evaluated on. A cell needs at least three items. We report the mean over language settings of the per-setting agreement, so that settings with more evaluated items do not dominate the result.

\textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} shows that a 350M proxy recovers the observed language-list ranking across the three settings. The 350M proxy agrees with the reference on 100\% of the shared languages at $L = 8$, 15 and 30, and the 600M proxy on 100, 83 and 100\%. The 175M proxy agrees at $L = 8$ but gets the decision backwards at $L = 15$ (17\%) and $L = 30$ (4\%). The 1B proxy, which exists only against the 1.7B reference at $L = 8$ and 30, agrees on 100\% at $L = 8$ but only 31\% at $L = 30$. The disagreement at $L = 30$ warrants caution: the effect of the list decision on bits per byte at the 1.7B reference is 0.58 seed standard deviations relative to the pooled baseline seed variability (\S\ref{subsec:rq4}). This small effect makes its sign potentially sensitive to training randomness. At $L = 8$ (1.6 seed standard deviations) and $L = 15$ (2.7), 350M also matches the reference, but repeated reference runs would be needed to establish the stability of those rankings.

\textbf{Training progress.} Within the available reference runs, BPB rankings stabilize early. The reference's ranking at 20\% of its run already matches its final ranking on 100\% of the languages for the list decision and on 100\% for the depth decision, and agreement remains high through 80\%. The later checkpoints preserve these observed rankings; this does not establish that the same stopping point would suffice in a new run. The one exception is the smallest proxy, which reverses its answer over its own run: at 175M the list decision agrees with the reference on 86\% of languages at 20\% of training and on 40\% at the end, so the 175M final checkpoint is a worse proxy than its own early checkpoint. We hypothesize that this is the optimizer-timescale effect of \S\ref{subsec:models} reaching the 175M rung, whose run is shorter than the optimizer's memory.

\textbf{Architecture and benchmark decisions.} The depth intervention has an effect of 1.1 to 2.4 pooled baseline seed standard deviations at the reference. The 175M proxy has 0\% agreement and the 350M proxy 51\%, but these agreement scores alone cannot distinguish a systematic rank reversal from an unstable reference ranking. Benchmark agreement ranges from 0.37 to 0.58 across the reported comparisons, including comparisons of earlier reference checkpoints with their final checkpoints. We find no consistent improvement with proxy size or training progress in these aggregate results. \S\ref{subsec:rq3} asks whether task-level statistics can nevertheless identify more reliable comparisons.

\subsection{RQ3: Can we know cheaply whether the decision will be reliable?}
\label{subsec:rq3}

\textbf{Finding.} Proxy statistics correlate with decision accuracy, but do not certify an individual decision. The relative-standard-deviation SNR has a Spearman correlation of 0.4 to 0.6 on benchmarks and 0.5 to 0.7 on per-language BPB. Agreement between early and final proxy rankings performs similarly. The signal term accounts for most of the association observed for SNR.

\textbf{Setup.} Decision accuracy needs the reference model. We ask which statistics predict it, distinguishing statistics of a single proxy size from those requiring a scaling curve. For every task and proxy size we compute the signal-to-noise ratio under three of the definitions of \S\ref{subsec:methodology-snr} (relative standard deviation, discrepancy, and the standard deviation of pairwise distances), the signal and the noise on their own, the margin above chance, the coefficient of determination of the scaling fit of \S\ref{subsec:rq1}, and the agreement between the proxy's ranking at 20\% of its run and at its end. Each candidate is correlated (Spearman $\rho$) with the task's decision accuracy by size, the agreement between the ranking of all design variants at the proxy size and at the reference, computed by the pipeline of \citet{heineman_signal_2025} on the same cells. We report the correlation separately for benchmark tasks and for the per-language bits-per-byte tasks, at the 175M, 350M and 600M proxies.

\textbf{Results.} We evaluate the candidates on eligible tasks at each proxy size; correlations require defined values for both the candidate and decision accuracy. The draft reports 68, 78 and 93 benchmark tasks at 175M, 350M and 600M, and 101 BPB entries.

Decision accuracy against the 1B target is 0.73, 0.71 and 0.79 (median) for those benchmark tasks and 0.81, 0.89 and 0.89 for bits per byte, so there is variation to predict on both sides. The relative-standard-deviation SNR is consistently associated with decision accuracy: $\rho = 0.52$, 0.60 and 0.41 on benchmarks and 0.69, 0.60 and 0.52 on bits per byte. Its signal term alone is almost as good (0.48, 0.54, 0.36 and 0.62, 0.50, 0.48), while its noise term alone is at most 0.23 anywhere, suggesting that dispersion across design variants explains most of SNR's predictive association on this ladder. The discrepancy variant is uninformative on benchmarks (0.11, 0.16, $-0.01$) and undefined on bits per byte, and the margin above chance predicts little (0.27 at 175M, negative at 600M): distance above chance alone does not reliably rank tasks by decision accuracy.

\textbf{Within-proxy stability.} The proxy's own training curve provides another surrogate without a noise estimate. The agreement between a proxy's ranking of the variants at 20\% of its run and at its end correlates with its eventual decision accuracy at 0.33, 0.59 and 0.57 on benchmarks and 0.61, 0.62 and 0.55 on bits per byte, giving correlations comparable to SNR from 350M up. The coefficient of determination of the scaling fit is a weak predictor on bits per byte (0.10 to 0.22), where every language scales predictably, and a moderate one on benchmarks (0.21 to 0.48), where it separates the families of \S\ref{subsec:rq1}. These associations motivate checking both within-proxy ranking stability and separation between variants before committing to a larger run. They do not establish a calibrated threshold for accepting a decision. In particular, early-to-final agreement requires completing the proxy run, even though it does not require training the reference.

\subsection{RQ4: Does it depend on the design choice?}
\label{subsec:rq4}

\textbf{Finding.} Predictivity varies with the intervention. Sampling temperature and second-language choice change reference BPB by 3.4 to 4.9 pooled baseline seed standard deviations and yield high agreement at 350M. The depth and language-list effects are smaller, at 1.5 to 1.6. Benchmark effects lie between 1.1 and 1.8 and show weak aggregate agreement across interventions.

\textbf{Setup.} We compare intervention effects with the variability observed across repeated baseline runs. For each intervention and language setting we measure the absolute difference between the two levels at the reference, per item, in units of that item's seed standard deviation, taken as the median over the seed-replicated cells of the baseline (deep, scheme A) at the same task. A ratio near 1 means the observed difference is comparable to this baseline variability. The denominator pools seed-replicated cells; it is neither the standard error of the intervention contrast nor a direct estimate of variability at every reference size. We then read the decision accuracy of each proxy size at its final checkpoint, as in \S\ref{subsec:rq2}, for five pairwise comparisons from the four intervention types: depth, language lists, sampling temperature ($T = 1$ against $T = 3$ at $L = 50$), and the second language at $L = 2$ (Russian against Chinese, and against Spanish). The second-language decision has one shared trained language, English, so we instead evaluate its BPB decision on languages trained by neither level.

\textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:rq4}a compares intervention effects relative to baseline variability. On bits per byte, the median effect at the reference is 4.9 seed standard deviations for Russian against Chinese, 3.9 for sampling temperature and 3.4 for Russian against Spanish, against 1.6 for the language lists and 1.5 for depth. On benchmarks every intervention sits between 1.1 and 1.8. Figure~\ref{fig:rq4}b shows that the proxy's reliability follows the same order. The temperature decision is read correctly on 88\% of the trained languages by 175M and 96\% by 350M, against a 600M reference; the second-language decisions on 75 to 78\% at 175M and 86 to 90\% at 350M over the never-trained languages. For language selection, 350M matches the observed reference ranking (\S\ref{subsec:rq2}); the depth comparison shows no similarly consistent transfer. On aggregate training loss, the two architectures differ by about one seed standard deviation. This small separation makes training variability a plausible contributor to the architecture result, but does not establish the cause of the 175M rank reversal.

\textbf{Benchmark comparisons.} Agreement remains weak across interventions: between 0.30 and 0.54 at every proxy size for every decision, with no gain from a larger proxy. We therefore find no consistently reliable benchmark-based decision among these comparisons. We hypothesize that the larger effects relative to variability in BPB help explain its stronger agreement. Its dense token-level observations may contribute, but byte normalization alone does not guarantee low variance, and tokens within a document are not independent.

\subsection{RQ5: Can scaling estimates transfer across languages?}
\label{subsec:rq5}

\textbf{Finding.} Borrowing a scaling exponent from other languages supports prediction from a single 175M measurement of the target language. Median absolute relative error at the available reference is 3.4\% for trained languages and 6.7\% for languages absent from training. The language-list decision also transfers to languages absent from training, with 76\% agreement at 600M against 94\% on shared training languages. This tests transfer with limited target-language evaluation, rather than prediction without measuring that language.

\textbf{Setup.} Bits per byte is scored on all 100 validation languages for every cell, including 45 languages absent from all training mixtures represented in this analysis. A language can also be absent at one $L$ and present at another, so we classify training exposure separately for each (language, $L$) chain. On the deep, scheme-A ladder at each $L$ we fit, per language, $\log \mathrm{BPB} = a_\ell - \alpha_\ell \log N$ on the proxy rungs. We then hold each language out in turn and predict its reference bits per byte with the median exponent of the other 99 languages at that $L$ and an intercept fitted on its own $k$ smallest rungs, for $k$ from one (175M alone) to four. We compare the relative error at the reference against the language's own fit (which needs three rungs) and against taking the largest proxy's value as the prediction. The reference is 1.7B at $L \in \{1, 2, 8, 30\}$ and 1B at $L \in \{15, 50\}$; 106 (language, $L$) chains are trained languages and 494 are not. For decisions, we read the language-list decision of \S\ref{subsec:rq2} on the languages neither scheme trains.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rq5_transfer.pdf}
\caption{Transfer of scaling behaviour to held-out languages. (a) Median absolute relative error of the reference's bits per byte as a function of how many of the held-out language's smallest rungs are measured, with the exponent borrowed from the other languages (solid), fitted on the language itself (dashed, needs three rungs), or with no extrapolation at all (dotted), for languages present in or absent from the corresponding training mixture. (b) Predicted against observed reference bits per byte from a single 175M measurement per language.}
\label{fig:rq5}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:rq5} shows that pooling exponents across languages supports accurate extrapolation. The median exponent is 0.14 at $L = 1$, 0.16 to 0.17 at $L \in \{2, 8, 15, 30\}$ and 0.20 at $L = 50$, with a standard deviation across languages of 0.03 to 0.07. Consequently, the borrowed exponent with a single 175M measurement predicts the reference within a median 3.4\% for trained languages and 6.7\% for never-trained ones (Figure~\ref{fig:rq5}a), and 71\% of the never-trained predictions land within 10\%. For trained languages, adding measurements lowers the error to 2.1\% at three rungs, compared with 5.1\% for the language-specific fit. We hypothesize that pooling reduces sensitivity to noise in short scaling curves. The advantage is not uniform across training-exposure groups or numbers of measured rungs, Figure~\ref{fig:rq5}a also shows settings where the language-specific fit is competitive. The no-extrapolation baseline is 36 to 45\% off from one rung and still 6.5 to 7.3\% off from four. Never-trained languages are predicted with a small systematic bias (median signed error $-3.4$\%), under the convention $(\mathrm{predicted}-\mathrm{observed})/\mathrm{observed}$. The extrapolation therefore slightly underestimates their reference BPB and is optimistic about their performance. Panel (b) compares predictions with observations across a five-fold range of BPB; the identity line makes the direction of the residuals visible.

\textbf{Decision transfer.} Transferring a design ranking is less consistent than predicting BPB. On the languages neither scheme trains, the list decision is read correctly by 56\% at 175M, 68\% at 350M and 76\% at 600M and 1B, against 40, 100, 94 and 65\% on the trained languages. Agreement is higher on shared training languages at 350M and 600M, but this ordering reverses at 175M and 1B. Different proxy sizes also cover different language settings and references. These results support partial transfer of the language-list decision, rather than a general advantage for either language population. We leave the question of which language properties predict the residual to future work.
