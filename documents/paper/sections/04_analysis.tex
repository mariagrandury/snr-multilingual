\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We analyze the task scores $Y_{b_\ell}(\theta,S,f)$ as defined in \S\ref{sec:methodology}.
Throughout the analysis, we use these evaluation runs as the basic observations and aggregate them differently depending on the question: across model sizes to study scaling with size, across checkpoints to study training trajectories, and across architectures, mixtures, and seeds to assess robustness.
% We organize the analysis as three questions, in the order a developer asks them. \S\ref{subsec:rq0}: at which per-language training-token counts does an evaluation produce a score distinguishable from its own noise? \S\ref{subsec:rq2}: among the evaluations that pass this test, at which proxy sizes and training fractions does the ranking of model variants at the proxy point agree with their ranking at the reference point? \S\ref{subsec:rq3}: which statistics computed from the proxy runs alone predict that agreement?
We organize the analysis around three components. \S\ref{subsec:rq0} examines when evaluation scores become reliable as training increases, \S\ref{subsec:rq2} measures when smaller or earlier evaluations recover the final ranking of model variants, and \S\ref{subsec:rq3} investigates which proxy statistics predict this ranking agreement.

\subsection{Measurability: At which size and token counts are multilingual benchmark tasks above chance?}
\label{subsec:rq0}

\begin{rqfinding}
(These blocks are for internal alignment, we will probably remove them before
submission)
\\ \\
\textbf{1. Many of the commonly used multilingual tasks are at chance on our ladder sizes.} Of the benchmark tasks in trained languages, only 40\% are above chance at 90M and still only 64\% at 1.7B.
\\ \\
\textbf{2. The answer format, not the training tokens, decides which tasks pass.} Scoring the answer text (acc\_norm) instead of a letter lifts Belebele from 4 to 85 of its 105 languages at 1.7B, Global-MMLU from 1 to 35 of 37 and INCLUDE from 2 to 31 of 43. The letter-format INCLUDE stays mostly at chance however many tokens of the language the model has seen.
\\ \\
%3. The item count sets the bar. The margin a run needs over chance ranges from +0.006 for the largest tasks to +0.157 for the smallest.
\textbf{3. Above chance, benchmark accuracy scales predictably with model size.} A log-linear fit in model size explains most of the variance of a typical task ($R^2 = 0.87$), and most tasks that scale predictably with size also do so along training (311 of 368, 85\%).
\end{rqfinding}

We first ask whether each benchmark provides measurable signal at the model scales considered.
For each benchmark-language task $b_\ell$ and model size $N$, we compare performance against the corresponding chance level, calculated as explained in \S\ref{subsec:evaluation}.
We test how much of the result depends on the answer format, and relate it to the training tokens of each language. We then ask whether the tasks above chance also scale predictably.

\textbf{Above chance per size.} 
The models on our ladder are at chance on a large part of the evaluation suite. Of the 933 analysed tasks with a chance level in trained languages, 40\% are above chance at 90M and 64\% at 1.7B, while 34\% never pass at any size (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_chance_considered_counts}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_gate_and_curves}). Training one rung further moves this frontier: on the six configurations trained at 3B, the number of tasks above chance grows from 612 to 778 of 933 (\S\ref{subsec:rq2}).
The tasks at chance concentrate in popular benchmarks that ask for the letter of the correct option. Up to 1.7B, the letter-format Belebele, Global-MMLU and INCLUDE are above chance in only 3 of 49, 1 of 29 and 2 of 36 trained languages, and MMLU and CommonsenseQA are at chance in English. Most of these languages pass only at 3B (45, 28 and 31 more, and English for MMLU and CommonsenseQA). Among the screened benchmarks, MMMLU stays at chance in all 12 trained languages and MMLU (Okapi) in 26 of 27, even at 3B.
In contrast, benchmarks scored on the answer text pass early. HellaSwag (Okapi) and MultiBLiMP are above chance at 90M in every trained language, and so are 42 of the 49 Belebele languages once the letters are removed. A few benchmarks stay at chance at every size up to 3B whatever the format: TruthfulQA (MC2), ToxiGen, and the cloze versions of BBH and ACP-Bench. This suggests that these benchmarks measure abilities beyond the scale of our ladder, while for the letter-format benchmarks the bottleneck is the answer format, which we examine next.
To select the tasks used in the analyses, we considered \ntasksconsidered tasks. Of the 1,454 among them with a chance level, 61\% pass at 1.7B (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq00_pass_counts_considered}).
The full grid per benchmark and language is in Figure~\ref{fig:app_chance_full}. 


\textbf{Effect of answer format.}
Most of the tasks at chance expect the letter of the correct option as answer. This requires the model to map an answer label onto its content, an ability that small models may lack independently of whether they know the answer \citeneeded{}. %\citep{khatun_study_2024, madaan_quantifying_2024}.
To isolate this effect, we adapt these tasks so that the model scores the textual answer options directly.
We apply two approaches. The programmatic reformatting removes the letters, leaving the text of every item unchanged.
For the model-based reformulation, we prompt \texttt{gemini-3.8-flash} \citep{gemini_team_gemini_2024} to restate each question as a declarative stem and each option as a statement completing it, without revealing the correct option.
At 1.7B, the programmatic version raises the number of languages above chance from 4 to 85 of 105 for Belebele, from 1 to 35 of 37 for Global-MMLU and from 2 to 31 of 43 for INCLUDE. The model-based version passes in every trained Belebele language at every size. In INCLUDE, 31 of 43 languages pass, and the gains widen with model size, from 0.015 at 175M to 0.075 at 1.7B. We inspect the reformulated items and verify that the rewrite introduces no length cue, as the correct option is the single longest in 22.2\% of original items and 21.9\% of rewritten ones against 25\% by chance. Detailed results by benchmark are in Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_task_reformulation} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_task_reformulation}) and Figure~\ref{fig:app_chance_reformulation} (Appendix~\ref{app:chance-threshold}).

\textbf{Above chance per training tokens.}
Model size is not the only factor that determines whether a task is above chance, since a model must also see enough text in the task's language. We place each evaluation cell (a task, model size, language setting and checkpoint) at the number of tokens of its language that the checkpoint has seen. Outside English, 42\% of the cells with fewer than $10^{8}$ tokens are above chance, 55\% of those between $10^{8}$ and $10^{10}$, and 64\% beyond $10^{10}$. A third of the cells therefore stay at chance even after ten billion tokens of the language. Ten billion tokens is the full Chinchilla budget ($20N$) of a 500M model spent on a single language, and 29\% of the Chinchilla budget of our 1.7B model. The benchmarks differ in when they gain. HellaSwag (Okapi), MultiBLiMP and TokSuite, which test language modelling and grammar, are above chance in nearly every cell below $10^{8}$ tokens, as is the LLM-reformulated Belebele. Most of the others climb between $10^{8}$ and $10^{10}$ tokens, and the climb follows the answer format. On the same items, Belebele RF rises from 76\% to 91\% of its cells while the original stays at 3--10\%, and Global-MMLU RF rises from 38\% to 88\% while the original stays at 6--10\%. Knowledge tied to the language's own exams needs more exposure than translated knowledge: INCLUDE RF reaches only 53\% between $10^{8}$ and $10^{10}$ tokens, where Global-MMLU RF is at 88\%, and reaches 99\% only beyond. ARC (Okapi), a science QA benchmark, gains latest, from 31\% of its cells between $10^{8}$ and $10^{10}$ tokens to 92\% beyond, while XNLI and the English INCLUDE v2 RF level off at 76--82\%. Beyond $10^{10}$ tokens, the cells cover only Spanish, Chinese, Russian and German at 350M--1.7B.
This effect interacts with model size. Between $10^{8}$ and $10^{9}$ tokens, 43\% of the 90M cells are above chance against 62\% of the 1.7B cells, and the 1.7B cells gain nothing beyond $10^{10}$ tokens (66\% in both higher bins). Part of the rise across bins therefore comes from larger models filling the high bins: weighting every size equally, the share rises from 45\% to 54\% and 63\%. This suggests that exposure to the language is necessary but not sufficient, and that the token count a benchmark needs depends on the model size (Table~\ref{tab:rq0_thresholds}).
The answer format matters here too. The original letter-format tasks fail the filter at nearly every token count reached in the sweep: pooled over the model sizes, the original INCLUDE stays at 3--17\% of its cells in every token bin from $10^{7}$ on, while its reformulated version reaches 99--100\% from $10^{10}$ tokens on (Figure~\ref{fig:rq0}, where a single size reaches 44\% of the nine cells of one bin).
A small set of tasks passes at very small token counts and fails at intermediate ones. We test two candidate causes on the eleven tasks of this kind: a preference for the longest option at early checkpoints, and training tokens of related languages (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq00_early_passes}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_gate_and_curves}). Most passes come from a constant answer instead. In 96\% of the passing runs, the option the model picks most often would clear chance on its own, because the answer keys of these small tasks are unbalanced. The longest option explains Japanese PAWS-X, and possibly the Malayalam task of the reformatted m-MMLU, whose runs pick the longest option more often than it holds the answer (0.38 against 0.28). On the other nine tasks, runs that never trained the language or a related one pass about as often at the same size and checkpoint, and on the Malayalam task more often.
The full grid per benchmark is in Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_chance_vs_train_tokens} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_chance_vs_train_tokens}).

\textbf{Token thresholds per benchmark.}
Table~\ref{tab:rq0_thresholds} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_chance_vs_train_tokens}) turns these curves into a practical guide. For each benchmark, answer format and model size, it gives the training tokens of a language at which 50\% and 90\% of the evaluation cells in that language are above chance. Three groups emerge. First, HellaSwag, MultiBLiMP and the LLM-reformulated Belebele reach 90\% from the fewest tokens any size saw, so they are measurable in every trained language from the start. Second, the letter-format originals of Belebele, Global-MMLU and INCLUDE, together with BLEnD, CulturalBench-hard and TruthfulQA (MC2), never reach 50\% at any size, whatever the tokens of the language. Their reformulated versions do. At 1.7B, the RF version of Belebele is above 50\% from the fewest tokens and reaches 90\% at 9M tokens, Global-MMLU RF reaches 90\% at 210M, and INCLUDE RF reaches 50\% at 150M and 90\% at 4.9B. Third, for the remaining benchmarks the threshold falls with model size. INCLUDE RF needs 1.3B tokens of the language for 50\% of its cells at 90M and 150M at 1.7B, and ARC (Okapi) reaches 50\% only from 600M on, at 3.3B tokens, against 320M at 1.7B. A 1.7B model therefore needs about ten times fewer tokens of a language than a 90M one before the same benchmark leaves chance.

\textbf{Item-count dependence of the filter.} Whether a task can pass also depends on its size, since the margin a task needs above chance shrinks with its item count. The median required margins are 9.00 percentage points for nonparallel Global PIQA at 100 items per language, 2.44 for Belebele at 900, 0.74 for HellaSwag at about 9{,}000 and 0.60 for Global-MMLU at about 14{,}000. Over the six sizes from 90M to 1.7B, these families pass the filter in 37.5\%, 1.0\%, 100.0\% and 3.6\% of their cells, and the reformulated Belebele and Global-MMLU in 74.0\% and 72.1\% (Appendix~\ref{app:chance-threshold}). A family with few items is reported as unable to detect small departures from chance, not as unmeasurable.

\textbf{Random definition in the filter.} To test the robustness of the filter, which compares every task with uniform guessing, we compute it also using the majority class as the chance level. We find that for the tasks with fixed answer labels it lowers the share above chance by 1.5 to 2.8 percentage points, depending on the size (Appendix~\ref{app:majority_chance}).

\textbf{Benchmark scaling behavior.}
\label{subsec:rq1} % label kept here so existing \ref{subsec:rq1} citations resolve
A task above chance carries signal at a given size, but this does not mean that its scores change in a consistent way. For the cells above the chance filter, we therefore measure how predictably benchmark performance changes with model size and training progress.
Measurements without a chance level, per-language bits per byte in particular, are not gated, so for them this test of consistent change with model size and training tokens is the only check of measurability.
For model size scaling, we fit final-checkpoint performance against $\log_{10}N$ across model sizes on the deep, scheme-A ladder at seed 1904, one fit per language setting, and require at least three model sizes.
For training scaling, we fit performance against log training tokens within each model, requiring at least five checkpoints.
For each task, we summarize the resulting fits across mixtures that train its language, requiring at least two fits on each axis.
We report $R^2$ as a measure of scaling predictability and Spearman's $\rho$ as a measure of monotonicity and direction.
For BPB and bBPB, we reverse the sign of $\rho$ so that positive values consistently indicate improvement.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(a) shows that benchmarks differ substantially in how predictably they scale with model size. Above chance, the median size-fit $R^2$ is 0.87 over 389 benchmark tasks and 1.00 for the per-language BPB of the 30 languages trained at two or more settings (Appendix~\ref{app:rq01_scaling_predictability}). HellaSwag, LAMBADA, and BPB have median size-fit $R^2$ values of 0.88, 0.96, and 1.00, respectively, whereas the letter-format Global-MMLU, INCLUDE, and Belebele have no fit at all on the scaling ladder considered here, because they stay at chance wherever a fit is possible. Their reformulated versions fit at family medians of 0.84--0.92 (Appendix~\ref{app:rq01_scaling_predictability}, with the curves in Figures~\ref{fig:app_size_scaling} and~\ref{fig:app_training_scaling}). The direction of scaling also matters, since high predictability does not necessarily correspond to improvement. For example, the Spanish TruthfulQA-Multi task has a median size-fit $R^2$ of 0.97 but $\rho=-0.75$ on the sizes where it is above chance (90M--350M), decreasing with model size at three of its four language settings and showing why $R^2$ alone is insufficient.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(b) compares scaling predictability across model size and training. Some benchmark evaluations can be well predicted along both dimensions, while others can be predicted across model size but not across training checkpoints. We call a task predictable along an axis when its median $R^2$ is at least 0.5 (and, across size, its median $\rho$ is not negative). Of the 419 accuracy and BPB tasks with fits on both axes, 311 (74\%) are predictable along both. We find no accuracy or BPB task that is predictable across training checkpoints but not across model size, and only 9 of the 688 bBPB tasks are.
This suggests that, for the measurements that exhibit predictable scaling in our setting, varying model size may provide useful signal without requiring long training trajectories.
In other words, in our setting, comparing larger models earlier in training appears more informative than training smaller models for longer.

\subsection{Agreement: How small and early can a proxy be while preserving the reference decision?}
\label{subsec:rq2}

\begin{rqfinding}
\textbf{1. A fully trained smaller model ranks the design variants close to chance on benchmark accuracy.} Against the 1.7B reference it agrees on only 51--56\% of the decisions, from 90M to 1B.
\\
\\
\textbf{2. Training a smaller proxy longer does not close the gap.} Its ranking converges to its own final ranking during training, but its agreement with the 1.7B ranking stays flat at every checkpoint.
\\
\\
\textbf{3. Per-language BPB ranks like the reference where accuracy does not.} It recovers 89--94\% of the reference decisions from every proxy size except 600M (74\%).
\end{rqfinding}

Measurability alone does not make a proxy useful for development, as the proxy must also rank the design variants like the reference.
We next ask how early in model development we can recover the decisions that would be obtained from a full evaluation.
We measure decision accuracy (Eq.~\ref{eq:decision-accuracy}) in the three ranking-agreement settings of Table~\ref{tab:ra_defs}: DA-size for RA-size, DA-ckpt for RA-checkpoint and DA-goal for RA-end-to-end.
The pairs are formed among the seed-1904 runs of every language setting, architecture and data build, from 90M to 1.7B, and the reference is the final checkpoint of the 1.7B run of each configuration.
The analysis includes only the cells retained by the above-chance filter of \S\ref{subsec:rq0}, at the proxy and at the reference, together with the bBPB of every benchmark task, which has no chance level.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} considers the single-axis pairs and further keeps the tasks whose median DA-size or median DA-ckpt is at least 0.66 (\S\ref{subsec:methodology-da}). This filter selects on decision accuracy, so the figure is conditional on it. The unfiltered population, every gated task and every pair, is in Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_decision_accuracy} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}), and choosing the reliable tasks out of sample on half of the pairs moves DA-size by at most 0.02 against choosing them in sample on the same half, and puts the black line of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a 0.03--0.05 lower (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_rq2_crossfit}).

We study decision reliability along two dimensions of model development: training progress and model scale, and analyze it from three complementary perspectives. First, we measure within-model training convergence by comparing the decision at each intermediate checkpoint to the decision made by the final checkpoint of the same model size, testing how early a model's eventual decision stabilizes during training (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}b). Second, we measure full-scale decision prediction by comparing every intermediate checkpoint, across model sizes, to the fully trained largest model, testing how early smaller and partially trained models can recover the decision of the final reference model (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c). Third, we study scale convergence by comparing the final checkpoint of each model size to the final checkpoint of the largest model, quantifying how small a model can be while still reproducing decisions made at full scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a).

\textbf{Within-model training convergence.}
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}b shows that decision reliability increases steadily over training when each checkpoint is compared to the final checkpoint of the same model.
Early checkpoints recover only around 62--68\% of the final decisions, while agreement rises substantially later in training, to 83--93\% at 4.5C (86--93\% for the proxies below 1.7B), with smaller models generally stabilizing earlier than larger ones.
Part of this rise is persistence within a run rather than a decision. Two seeds of one configuration, which have nothing to decide, already agree on 78--80\% of the pairs at 90\% of training, against 80--84\% for real design pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_seed_uncertainty}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}).

\textbf{Predicting full-scale decisions from cheaper models.}
Figures~\ref{fig:rq2}a and~\ref{fig:rq2}c show that this within-model convergence does not translate into reliable prediction of the final 1.7B decisions.
Across fully trained smaller models, agreement with the 1.7B reference remains modest and non-monotonic with scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a), and additional training of smaller models yields only limited improvement in full-scale decision reliability (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c).
Without the reliability filter, on benchmark accuracy alone, DA-size is only 0.49--0.52 from 90M to 1B on the single-axis pairs and 0.51--0.56 on all design pairs (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq02_definition_sensitivity}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}).
On all design pairs, per-language BPB of the 50 trained languages recovers 89--94\% of the reference decisions from every proxy size except 600M (74\%), and on the single-axis pairs of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} 87--92\% except 600M (57\%), and 98\% or more at every size, 600M included, on the pairs the reference separates by more than two seed standard deviations (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq02_definition_sensitivity}). At 600M the deep seed-1904 runs sit about one seed standard deviation above their own scaling trend (seed 1904 is the worst of the three seeds at $K=2$ and $K=50$), which reverses the depth and activation pairs, whose 1.7B gap is itself only about one seed standard deviation (Appendix~\ref{app:rq03_noise_and_snr}).
Together, these results suggest that the cross-scale gap cannot be closed by training cheaper proxy models longer alone.


\textbf{Sensitivity analysis.}
Before reading these levels as properties of the proxies, we check that they do not depend on how DA is computed (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq02_definition_sensitivity}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}).
Counting a tied pair as one half raises benchmark-accuracy DA-size by about 0.03, and keeping only the pairs whose 1.7B difference exceeds two checkpoint standard deviations of the reference raises it to 0.55--0.62 while dropping half of the pairs. Per-language BPB never ties, and the same filter lifts it to 0.98--1.00 at every proxy except 600M (0.93).
Chance agreement is measured cell by cell by shuffling the proxy scores. Pooled over decisions, it sits at 0.49 for the benchmark tasks and their bBPB twins, so their observed DA-size of 0.57--0.58 is only 0.08--0.09 above it, and only 10--12\% of the benchmark cells beat their own null, while per-language BPB reads 0.79--0.93 against a null of 0.50 (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_permutation_null}).
Restricting to the pairs that the 1.7B reference separates by more than one or two seed standard deviations raises the pooled DA-size on the benchmark tasks and their bBPB twins to 0.63--0.65 and 0.69--0.73, but these pairs are only 52\% and 25\% of the comparable ones (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decisive_pairs}).
The reliability filter of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} raises the level of the lines, but its selection effect is small: chosen out of sample, the reliable tasks move DA-size by at most 0.02 (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_rq2_crossfit}), and holding the task set fixed moves the all-pair lines by at most 0.002 and the design-axis lines by at most 0.01 (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_fixed_rq2}, Appendix~\ref{app:fixed_populations}).


\textbf{Effect of evaluation protocol on DA.}
In \S\ref{subsec:rq0}, the answer format decided whether a task is measurable. The way a benchmark is scored also changes how its decisions transfer across sizes (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_bbpb}). On the reliable tasks of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}, accuracy-based DA-size is higher at larger proxies, from 0.56 at 90M to 0.69 at 1B, while bBPB stays at 0.60--0.65 at every size, so the flat line of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a is mostly that of the bBPB twins.
Over every gated task, the bBPB of a proxy predicts the 1.7B accuracy ranking at 0.58--0.59 from any proxy size, against 0.50--0.56 for accuracy itself, a gain of 0.10 at 90M and 0.03 at 1B (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}).
On the tasks where accuracy is still at chance at the proxy, bBPB ranks the design variants like the 1.7B bBPB at 0.60--0.63, against 0.46--0.50 for accuracy on the same tasks, but stays far below the 0.95--0.98 of the macro average of per-language BPB on the same pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_bbpb_acc_gate}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}). Scoring these items by log-likelihood therefore lifts their ranking above chance, but not to the reliability of BPB.

\textbf{Per-language BPB and the tokens of the language.}
Unlike benchmark accuracy, per-language BPB does not need the token thresholds of \S\ref{subsec:rq0} to agree with the reference. The 90M proxies already recover 90\% of the 1.7B decisions at the first tenth of their run, after about $8\times10^{6}$ tokens of a language, and 175M recovers 94\% at its final checkpoint. The 600M proxies stay at 69--74\% over their whole run, while 350M and 1B climb to 89\% and 92\% (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_da_vs_train_tokens}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_da_vs_train_tokens}).

\textbf{Reference choice.}
All of these values are measured against the 1.7B reference, which is a single run per configuration, so measured DA compares the proxy ranking to one realization of the reference ranking. The decisive-pair filter above bounds this effect from one side. At 1B, the only size above 600M with three seeds, two seeds of the same English designs agree with each other on only 53--60\% of the decisions pooled over tasks, about as often as the proxies agree with the 1.7B reference (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_seed_uncertainty}). With the 1B models as the reference, the seed of the reference alone moves the pooled single-axis DA-size of a proxy by up to 0.13, more than replacing the 1B reference with the 1.7B one (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq10_reference_choice}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq10_size_generalisation}).

\textbf{Extending the reference to 3B.}
A follow-up question is whether 1.7B is large enough to serve as the reference. We train six of the configurations one rung further, to 3B at $K \in \{8, 15, 30, 50\}$, and use these models as the reference (Appendix~\ref{app:rq10_size_generalisation}). The main conclusions hold. Per-language BPB carries over: the 1.7B models predict the 3B decisions at 0.94--0.95, every proxy from 90M reaches at least 0.78, and the macro average of BPB orders every pair the same way at 1.7B and at 3B. Pooled over decisions, benchmark accuracy reads 0.48--0.53 against either reference on the same decisions, so its gap to BPB does not come from the choice of the 1.7B reference. The larger reference also measures more of the suite. The above-chance filter keeps 778 of 933 benchmark tasks at 3B against 612 at 1.7B, mostly the letter-format originals of Belebele, Global-MMLU and INCLUDE, which their reformulations (\S\ref{subsec:rq0}) already make measurable at 1.7B. With six configurations, only the number of languages clears the minimum of three pairs per design axis at 3B, as the data scheme varies in two pairs only.

\subsection{Anticipation: Can cheap proxy statistics predict decision accuracy?}
\label{subsec:rq3}

\begin{rqfinding}
\textbf{1. No single surrogate leads at every proxy size.} Over every pair of design variants, early-checkpoint ranking agreement leads at four of the five proxy sizes, closely followed by the relative-standard-deviation SNR, both with a moderate association with DA-size ($\rho \approx 0.6$) that is already present at 90M.
\\
\\
\textbf{2. The predictive part of SNR is its noise term.} Signal alone and the margin above chance carry no positive information about decision accuracy on benchmark tasks.
\\
\\
\textbf{3. Pooled correlations hide large differences between benchmarks, scorings and languages.} The same SNR tracks decision accuracy closely within HellaSwag, more strongly on the bBPB twins than on accuracy, and more weakly on per-language BPB, where the SNR definitions reverse sign at 600M.
\end{rqfinding}


As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:rq2}, decision accuracy (DA) measures whether the ranking of models with different development decisions agrees with their ordering at reference scale.
\textcolor{blue}{However, DA is inherently retrospective, meaning that computing it requires both the proxy ranking and the ranking of the larger reference models.}
Ideally, we would instead like a \textcolor{blue}{\textit{surrogate metric} that can be computed from the proxy models alone and approximates the DA that would be obtained against the reference.}
Prior work has proposed several such diagnostics, most notably signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) \citep{heineman_signal_2025}, which compares the separation between model variants to the variability of their evaluation scores.
These methods have primarily been studied in English settings and at scales where downstream benchmark signal is already measurable.
In this experiment, we investigate first whether existing surrogate metrics remain predictive of DA in the multilingual setting and second, which surrogate provides a reliable signal at the smallest model scale.

We first test whether statistics proposed to characterize evaluation reliability can serve as surrogates for DA without requiring the reference model.
\textcolor{blue}{For each benchmark-language task and proxy size, we compute each surrogate using only information available at the proxy scale and measure its Spearman ($\rho$) association with DA}, i.e., agreement between the proxy-size ranking and the ranking at the 1.7B reference model.
We compare several families of surrogate statistics that can be computed without the reference model, including scaling-fit \(R^2\), SNR-based measures \citep{heineman_signal_2025} and their signal/noise components, margin above chance, and early-checkpoint ranking agreement. Scaling-fit \(R^2\) and the margin above chance build on the measurability analysis of \S\ref{subsec:rq0}, and early-checkpoint agreement on the training convergence of \S\ref{subsec:rq2}.
Scaling-fit \(R^2\) measures how well benchmark performance follows a log-linear trend across the model sizes available up to the proxy point. Margin above chance measures how far the benchmark score is above random-guessing performance. Early-checkpoint ranking agreement measures how well the ranking of model variants at an early training checkpoint matches their ranking at the final checkpoint of the same model size.
Appendix~\ref{app:surrogates} defines the SNR variants and the other surrogates.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} shows the Spearman ($\rho$) association with DA of each surrogate across proxy sizes over the benchmark tasks that pass the above-chance filter, scored by accuracy, and the bBPB twins of every benchmark task, and thus whether any surrogate remains informative across scales. Here and in the rest of this section, DA is taken over every pair of design variants, the single-axis pairs included. On the single-axis pairs of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} alone, the correlations are weaker and the relative-standard-deviation SNR leads at every proxy size ($\rho = 0.37$--$0.46$, against $0.24$--$0.36$ for early-checkpoint agreement, Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq04_surrogates_mono_axis} in Appendix~\ref{app:rq04_surrogates}).

\textbf{Surrogate predictivity in the multilingual setting.}
We find that \textcolor{blue}{no single surrogate consistently predicts decision accuracy} across the multilingual settings of our model ladder.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} shows that among the simple candidates, early-checkpoint ranking agreement and the relative-standard-deviation SNR provide the strongest overall association with DA ($\rho = 0.54$--$0.62$ and $0.53$--$0.60$ across proxy sizes), \textcolor{blue}{suggesting that benchmarks whose ranking settles early in training and whose scores vary little across checkpoints are also more likely to preserve the ranking of model variants across scales.} Scaling-fit \(R^2\) follows at $\rho = 0.45$--$0.49$. Signal alone ($\rho = -0.16$ to $0.00$) and the margin above chance ($\rho = -0.03$ to $0.09$) carry no positive information about DA.
For SNR-based surrogates, however, performance depends strongly on how noise is defined, with some noise estimates yielding substantially stronger associations than others. For example, in the catalogue analysis of Appendix~\ref{app:sur-results}, which pools the proxy sizes and reads one point per (benchmark, language) pair, replacing checkpoint-based noise with $k$-fold benchmark noise lowers the association of the relative-standard-deviation SNR with DA from \(\rho=0.75\) to \(\rho=0.21\) (Table~\ref{tab:sur-corr-snr}).
The aggregate results in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} pool all benchmark-language tasks that pass the chance threshold. When we stratify the analysis by benchmark or multilingual training regime, however, the relationships differ by stratum.
While the strongest SNR association in the pooled catalogue is \(\rho=0.75\), the best SNR definition reaches \(\rho=0.93\) for HellaSwag but only \(\rho=0.70\) on the tasks in the eight languages of the $K = 8$ list and \(\rho=0.69\) for per-language BPB (Table~\ref{tab:sur-corr-strata}).
These values are \(0.18\) higher, \(0.05\) lower, and \(0.06\) lower than the pooled association, respectively.
Table~\ref{tab:sur-corr-strata} (Appendix~\ref{app:sur-results}) gives the surrogate-DA Spearman correlations per benchmark, language tier and proxy size, and Appendix~\ref{app:rq04_surrogates} the per-language correlations of every SNR definition.

\textbf{At what model scale does surrogate signal emerge?}
\textcolor{blue}{We next ask how early in model development a surrogate becomes informative about DA.}
Using the same analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3}, we compare surrogate-DA associations across increasing proxy model sizes, from 90M to 1B.
This allows us to test whether useful surrogate signal is already present at the smallest scale and whether it strengthens or remains stable as model scale increases.
\textcolor{blue}{At the smallest proxy scale, the two leading surrogates already reach a moderate association with DA ($\rho = 0.58$ and $0.55$ at 90M), and neither strengthens consistently as the proxy grows.}
Early-checkpoint ranking agreement is strongest at 175M ($\rho=0.62$), and its association changes little at 350M ($\rho=0.59$), indicating that this early signal is fairly consistent across scales.
By 350M, inverted relative-standard-deviation noise reaches $\rho=0.56$ and discrepancy-based SNR reaches $\rho=0.34$.
Scaling-fit $R^2$ becomes available only from 350M onward, where it reaches $\rho=0.49$ and stays at $\rho = 0.45$--$0.49$ up to 1B.
Overall, a moderate surrogate signal is present from the smallest proxy scale but does not strengthen with model size, and the surrogate providing the strongest association is early-checkpoint agreement at every size except 350M, where the relative-standard-deviation SNR leads.
Similar to our earlier findings on multilingual benchmark scaling, we find that decision surrogates behave less consistently than suggested by prior English-focused studies, with their usefulness depending strongly on the benchmark and the training-language regime, and for per-language BPB on the proxy size (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq04_surrogates_by_scoring}, right).

\textbf{Accuracy, bBPB and per-language BPB.}
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} pools the accuracy of each benchmark task with its bBPB twin. Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq04_surrogates_by_scoring} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq04_surrogates}) splits this pool by scoring and adds the per-language BPB.
The pooled level and the lead of early-checkpoint agreement come mainly from the bBPB twins. On the twins, early-checkpoint agreement reaches $\rho = 0.57$--$0.68$, against $0.28$--$0.39$ on accuracy.
On accuracy alone, the relative-standard-deviation SNR leads at 90M and 600M ($\rho = 0.39$ and $0.40$), ties early-checkpoint agreement at 175M ($\rho = 0.33$) and trails the scaling-fit $R^2$ at 350M ($\rho = 0.37$ against $0.38$), and at 1B the inverted noise ($\rho = 0.44$), the discrepancy-based SNR ($\rho = 0.42$) and early-checkpoint agreement ($\rho = 0.39$) overtake it.
Per-language BPB has no twin and behaves differently again. Every statistic but the inverted noise is positive from 90M to 350M and turns negative at 600M, where this proxy disagrees with the reference (\S\ref{subsec:rq2}), and early-checkpoint agreement stays negative at 1B.

\textbf{What a surrogate cannot see.}
Part of this inconsistency has a structural cause. Every surrogate statistic is computed from the proxy runs, so none contains information about the sign of the score difference at the reference size.
The 600M per-language BPB result of \S\ref{subsec:rq2} is the measured instance. Per-language BPB scales almost perfectly with model size (median $R^2 = 1.00$, \S\ref{subsec:rq0}), yet at 600M its DA-size against the 1.7B reference is 0.74, against 0.89--0.94 at the other proxy sizes.
The proxy statistics cannot flag this case, because it comes from the seed draw of the deep 600M runs on pairs whose 1.7B gap is about one seed standard deviation (\S\ref{subsec:rq2}).
At this size the SNR definitions, signal alone and the scaling-fit $R^2$ all turn negative ($\rho = -0.35$ to $-0.60$, Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq04_surrogates_by_scoring}, right), so a statistic that ranks the 600M proxy as reliable points to the wrong decisions.
Two follow-ups would test this limit directly: a held-out-language test of the token thresholds of \S\ref{subsec:rq0}, and a separation of within-proxy detectability from cross-scale agreement.
