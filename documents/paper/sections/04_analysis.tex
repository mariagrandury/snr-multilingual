\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We analyze the task scores $Y_{b_\ell}(\theta,S,f)$ as defined in §3.
Throughout the analysis, we use these evaluation runs as the basic observations and aggregate them differently depending on the question: across model sizes to study scaling with size, across checkpoints to study training trajectories, and across architectures, mixtures, and seeds to assess robustness.
Using these views, we ask which multilingual measurements track learning, when the rankings they induce transfer to larger models, and whether cheaper statistics can anticipate that transfer.

\subsection{RQ1: Which multilingual benchmark tasks scale predictably?}
\label{subsec:rq1}

We first ask whether each benchmark provides measurable signal at the model scales considered.
For each benchmark--language task $b_\ell$ and model size $N$, we compare performance against the corresponding chance level.
For the proxy-reference pairs above the chance gate, we measure how predictably benchmark performance changes with model size and training progress.
For model size scaling, we fit final-checkpoint performance against $\log_{10}N$ across model sizes, holding model configuration and seed fixed, and require at least three model sizes.
For training scaling, we fit performance against log training tokens within each model, requiring at least five checkpoints.
For each task, we summarize the resulting fits across mixtures that train its language, requiring at least two fits on each axis. % REVIEW
We report $R^2$ as a measure of scaling predictability and Spearman's $\rho$ as a measure of monotonicity and direction.
For BPB and training loss, we reverse the sign of $\rho$ so that positive values consistently indicate improvement.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(a) shows that benchmarks differ substantially in how predictably they scale with model size. HellaSwag, LAMBADA, and BPB have median size-fit $R^2$ values of 0.97, 0.99, and 0.95, respectively, whereas Global-MMLU, INCLUDE, and Belebele provide weak fits on the scaling ladder considered here. The direction of scaling also matters, since high predictability does not necessarily correspond to improvement. For example, TruthfulQA has a median size-fit $R^2$ of 0.73 but $\rho=-0.83$, indicating a strong and consistent decrease with model size and showing why $R^2$ alone is insufficient.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(b) compares scaling predictability across model size and training. Some benchmark evaluations can be well predicted along both dimensions, while others can be predicted across model size but not across training checkpoints. We find no cases where performance is predictable across training checkpoints but not across model size.
This suggests that, for the measurements that exhibit predictable scaling in our setting, varying model size may provide useful signal without requiring long training trajectories.
In other words, in our setting, comparing larger models earlier in training appears more informative than training smaller models for longer.

A remaining concern is that weak scaling may reflect the evaluation protocol rather than the underlying capability measured by the benchmark.
Multiple-choice tasks that present their options as lettered alternatives require the model to map an answer label onto its content, an ability that small models may lack independently of whether they know the answer \citep{}.
To isolate this effect, we reformulate the three benchmarks of Belebele, Global-MMLU, and INCLUDE so that the model scores the full answer options directly, rather than predicting their corresponding letter labels. 
We apply two reformulations. The first is programmatic: we remove the answer labels and score the four options directly, leaving the text of every item unchanged. 
The second is model-based: we prompt \texttt{gemini-3.8-flash} [CITE] to restate each question as a declarative stem and each option as a statement completing it, without revealing which option is correct.

Both reformulations substantially increase the number of benchmark--language subsets that pass the above-chance filter at 1.7B, from 11 to 86 of 105 for Belebele, from 0 to 35 of 37 for Global-MMLU, and from 4 to 31 of 43 for INCLUDE. Accuracy on the trained-language subsets rises by 0.098, 0.058, and 0.047, respectively, measured on the same models and checkpoints as the original items. The model-based reformulation, which we have so far completed for INCLUDE, is stronger still: 34 of 43 subsets pass the filter and accuracy rises by 0.075. Gains widen with model size, from 0.007 at 175M to 0.075 at 1.7B, and the rewrite introduces no length cue, as the correct option is the single longest in 23.2\% of original items and 23.3\% of rewritten ones against 25\% by chance. These results indicate that the weak scaling of these benchmarks is in large part a property of their answer format rather than of the capability they target: once the answer is scored as text, the same items separate models across the ladder.

We next ask how early in model development we can recover the decisions that would be obtained from a full evaluation. 
To isolate this question from benchmark quality itself, we restrict the analysis to benchmarks that already exhibit high decision reliability under full evaluation. The selection procedure and per-benchmark results are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:rq2_benchmarks}. 
We study decision reliability along two dimensions of model development: training progress and model scale, and analyze it from three complementary perspectives. First, we measure within-model training convergence by comparing the decision at each intermediate checkpoint to the decision made by the final checkpoint of the same model size, testing how early a model’s eventual decision stabilizes during training (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}b). Second, we measure full-scale decision prediction by comparing every intermediate checkpoint, across model sizes, to the fully trained largest model, testing how early smaller and partially trained models can recover the decision of the final reference model (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c). Third, we study scale convergence by comparing the final checkpoint of each model size to the final checkpoint of the largest model, quantifying how small a model can be while still reproducing decisions made at full scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a).

\textbf{Within-model training convergence.}
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}b shows that decision reliability increases steadily over training when each checkpoint is compared to the final checkpoint of the same model. 
Early checkpoints recover only around 50--60\% of the final decisions, while agreement rises substantially later in training, with smaller models generally stabilizing earlier than larger ones.

\textbf{Predicting full-scale decisions from cheaper models.}
Figures~\ref{fig:rq2}a and~\ref{fig:rq2}c show that this within-model convergence does not translate into reliable prediction of the final 1.7B decisions. 
Across fully trained smaller models, agreement with the 1.7B reference remains modest and non-monotonic with scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a), and additional training of smaller models yields only limited improvement in full-scale decision reliability (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c). 
Together, these results suggest that the cross-scale gap cannot be closed by training cheaper proxy models longer alone.


\subsection{RQ3: Can cheap proxy statistics predict decision accuracy?}
\label{subsec:rq3}


\textbf{Finding.} Proxy statistics correlate with ranking agreement, but do not certify an individual decision. The relative-standard-deviation SNR has a Spearman correlation of 0.4 to 0.6 on benchmarks and 0.5 to 0.7 on per-language BPB. Agreement between early and final proxy rankings performs similarly. The signal term accounts for most of the association observed for SNR.

\textbf{Setup.} Decision accuracy needs the reference model. We ask which statistics predict it, distinguishing statistics of a single proxy size from those requiring a scaling curve. For every task and proxy size we compute the signal-to-noise ratio under three of the definitions of \S\ref{subsec:methodology-snr} (relative standard deviation, discrepancy, and the standard deviation of pairwise distances), the signal and the noise on their own, the margin above chance, the coefficient of determination of the scaling fit of \S\ref{subsec:rq1}, and the agreement between the proxy's ranking at 20\% of its run and at its end. Each candidate is correlated (Spearman $\rho$) with the task's decision accuracy by size, the agreement between the ranking of all design variants at the proxy size and at the reference, computed by the pipeline of \citet{heineman_signal_2025} on the same cells. We report the correlation separately for benchmark tasks and for the per-language bits-per-byte tasks, at the 175M, 350M and 600M proxies.

Decision accuracy against the 1B target is 0.73, 0.71 and 0.79 (median) for those benchmark tasks and 0.81, 0.89 and 0.89 for bits per byte, so there is variation to predict on both sides. The relative-standard-deviation SNR is consistently associated with decision accuracy: $\rho = 0.52$, 0.60 and 0.41 on benchmarks and 0.69, 0.60 and 0.52 on bits per byte. Its signal term alone is almost as good (0.48, 0.54, 0.36 and 0.62, 0.50, 0.48), while its noise term alone is at most 0.23 anywhere, suggesting that dispersion across design variants explains most of SNR's predictive association on this ladder. The discrepancy variant is uninformative on benchmarks (0.11, 0.16, $-0.01$) and undefined on bits per byte, and the margin above chance predicts little (0.27 at 175M, negative at 600M): distance above chance alone does not reliably rank tasks by decision accuracy.

\textbf{Within-proxy stability.} The proxy's own training curve provides another surrogate without a noise estimate. The agreement between a proxy's ranking of the variants at 20\% of its run and at its end correlates with its eventual decision accuracy at 0.33, 0.59 and 0.57 on benchmarks and 0.61, 0.62 and 0.55 on bits per byte, giving correlations comparable to SNR from 350M up. The coefficient of determination of the scaling fit is a weak predictor on bits per byte (0.10 to 0.22), where every language scales predictably, and a moderate one on benchmarks (0.21 to 0.48), where it separates the families of \S\ref{subsec:rq1}. These associations motivate checking both within-proxy ranking stability and separation between variants before committing to a larger run. They do not establish a calibrated threshold for accepting a decision. In particular, early-to-final agreement requires completing the proxy run, even though it does not require training the reference.
