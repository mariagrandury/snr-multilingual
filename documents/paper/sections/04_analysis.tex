\subsection{Model Size and Token Count Thresholds}
\label{subsec:rq1_size_tokens} 

To select the tasks used in the analyses, we considered \ntasksconsidered tasks, including tasks from language-specific benchmarks and leaderboards (Table~\ref{tab:app_benchmarks_considered}).
\highlight{The models on our ladder are at chance for a high percentage of the evaluation suite.} Of the 933 analysed tasks with a chance level, only 40\% are above chance at 90M and 64\% at 1.7B, while 34\% never pass at any size (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_chance_shared}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_chance_threshold}). 
The tasks at chance concentrate in benchmarks that expect the letter of the correct option. Up to 1.7B, the letter-format reading and knowledge benchmarks Belebele, Global-MMLU and INCLUDE are above chance in only 3 of 49, 1 of 29 and 2 of 36 trained languages, and MMLU and CommonsenseQA are at chance in English. However, most of these languages pass only at 3B. % (45, 28 and 31 more, and English for MMLU and CommonsenseQA).
Among the screened benchmarks, MMMLU stays at chance in all 12 trained languages and MMLU (Okapi) in 26 of 27, even at 3B.
In contrast, benchmarks scored on the answer text pass at smaller sizes. HellaSwag (Okapi) and MultiBLiMP are above chance at 90M in every trained language. A few benchmarks stay at chance at every size up to 3B whatever the format: TruthfulQA (MC2), ToxiGen, BBH, and ACP-Bench.
This suggests that these benchmarks measure abilities beyond the scale of our ladder, while for the letter-format benchmarks the bottleneck is the answer format, which we further analyse in Section~\ref{subsec:rq1_factors}.
The full grid per benchmark and language is in Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_chance_full}. 


Model size is not the only factor that determines whether a task is above chance. We also ask how many target-language tokens a model needs to be trained on to pass the threshold.
Across non-English benchmarks, 42\% of the cells with fewer than 100M tokens of the language are above chance, 55\% of those between 100M and 10B, and 64\% beyond 10B. A third of the cells therefore stay at chance even after training on 10B tokens of language, the full Chinchilla budget of a 500M monolingual model.
HellaSwag (Okapi), MultiBLiMP and TokSuite, which test language modelling and grammar, are above chance in nearly every run below 100M tokens, while both ARC versions (Okapi and MT) need 10B tokens to pass chance.
Moreover, this effect interacts with model size. At 100M--1B tokens, 43\% of the 90M runs are above chance, compared to 62\% of the 1.7B runs. Therefore, \highlight{exposure to the language is necessary but not sufficient, and the token count a family needs depends on the model size} (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq0_thresholds}).

\subsection{Factors Influencing Chance Threshold}
\label{subsec:rq1_factors}

The expected answer format has a large impact on the model size and token count threshold at which evaluations start to perform at above chance. 
% The answer format also affects this analysis. The original letter-format tasks fail the filter at nearly every token count reached in the ladder: pooled over model sizes, the original INCLUDE stays at 3--17\% of its cells in every token bin from 10M on, while its reformulated version reaches 99--100\% at 10B (Figure~\ref{fig:rq0}).
\highlight{Most of the tasks at chance expect the letter of the correct option as answer.} This requires the model to map an answer label onto its content, an ability that small models may lack independently of whether they know the answer \citep{khatun_study_2024, madaan_quantifying_2024}.
To isolate this effect, we adapt these tasks so that the model scores the textual answer options directly.
We apply two approaches. The programmatic reformatting removes the letters, leaving the text of every item unchanged.
For the model-based reformulation, we prompt \texttt{gemini-3.8-flash} \citep{gemini_team_gemini_2024} to restate each question as a declarative stem and each option as a statement completing it, without revealing the correct option.
We inspect the reformulated items and verify that the rewrite introduces no length cue, as the correct option is the single longest in 22.2\% of original items and 21.9\% of rewritten ones against 25\% by chance.
At 1.7B, \highlight{the programmatic reformatting raises the number of languages above chance} from 5 to 86 of 105 for Belebele, from 1 to 35 of 37 for Global-MMLU and from 3 to 31 of 43 for INCLUDE. The model-based version passes in every trained Belebele language at every size. In INCLUDE, 32 of 43 languages pass, and the accuracy gains between original and reformatted versions widen with model size, from 0.015 at 175M to 0.075 at 1.7B.  Detailed results by benchmark are in Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_task_reformulation} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_task_reformulation}).

We find that for the tasks with fixed answer labels it lowers the share above chance by 1.5 to 2.8 percentage, depending on the size (Appendix~\ref{app:majority_chance}).

\subsection{Benchmark Scaling Behavior}
\label{subsec:rq1_scaling}
A task above chance carries signal at a given size, but this does not mean that its scores change in a consistent way. For the cells above the chance filter, we therefore measure how predictably benchmark performance changes with model size and training progress.
Measurements without a chance level, per-language bits per byte in particular, are not gated, so for them this test of consistent change with model size and training tokens is the only check of measurability.
For model size scaling, we fit final-checkpoint performance against $\log_{10}N$ across model sizes, holding model configuration and seed fixed, and require at least three model sizes.
For training scaling, we fit performance against log training tokens within each model, requiring at least five checkpoints.
For each task, we summarize the resulting fits across mixtures that train its language, requiring at least two fits on each axis. We report $R^2$ as a measure of scaling predictability and Spearman's $\rho$ as a measure of monotonicity and direction. For bBPB and BPB, we reverse the sign of $\rho$ so that positive values consistently indicate improvement.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(a) shows that benchmarks differ substantially in how predictably they scale with model size. \highlight{Above chance, the median size-fit $R^2$ is 0.87 over 389 benchmark tasks and 1.00 for the per-language BPB of the 30 languages trained at two or more settings} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq01_scaling_predictability}). HellaSwag, LAMBADA, and BPB have median size-fit $R^2$ values of 0.88, 0.96, and 1.00, respectively, whereas the letter-format Global-MMLU, INCLUDE, and Belebele have no fit at all on the scaling ladder considered here, because they stay at chance wherever a fit is possible. Their reformulated versions fit at family medians of 0.84--0.92 (Appendix~\ref{app:rq01_scaling_predictability}, with the curves in Figures~\ref{fig:app_rq01_size_scaling} and~\ref{fig:app_rq01_training_scaling}). The direction of scaling also matters, since high predictability does not necessarily correspond to improvement. For example, the Spanish TruthfulQA-Multi task has a median size-fit $R^2$ of 0.97 but $\rho=-0.75$ on the sizes where it is above chance (90M--350M), indicating a strong and consistent decrease with model size and showing why $R^2$ alone is an insufficient measure of scaling.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(b) compares scaling predictability across model size and training. Some benchmark evaluations can be well predicted ($R^2 \geq$ 0.5) along both dimensions, while others can be predicted across model size but not across training checkpoints. Of the 419 accuracy and BPB tasks with fits on both axes, 311 (74\%) are predictable along both. We find no accuracy or BPB task that is predictable across training checkpoints but not across model size, and only 9 of the 688 bBPB tasks are.
This suggests that, for the measurements that exhibit predictable scaling in our setting, varying model size may provide useful signal without requiring long training trajectories.
In other words, in our setting, \highlight{comparing larger models earlier in training appears more informative than training smaller models for longer}.

\textbf{Predicting full-scale decisions from cheaper models.}
Figures~\ref{fig:rq2}a and~\ref{fig:rq2}c show that this within-model convergence does not translate into reliable prediction of the final 1.7B decisions.
Across fully trained smaller models, agreement with the 1.7B reference remains modest and non-monotonic with scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a), and additional training of smaller models yields only limited improvement in full-scale decision reliability (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c).
Together, these results suggest that the cross-scale gap cannot be closed by training cheaper proxy models longer alone.


\textbf{Sensitivity analysis.}
We further test whether the DA results are driven by small performance differences between model variants (Appendix~\ref{app:da-sensitivity}). Treating near-tied reference scores as equivalent increases the absolute DA values but leaves the overall trends largely unchanged: ranking recovery improves with training progress, but not consistently with model size. At the same time, a complementary tie/difference categorization shows that larger models and later checkpoints are less likely to miss intervention effects that persist at the reference point, with \emph{tie--difference} cases decreasing and \emph{difference--difference} cases increasing. Together, these analyses suggest that exact ranking remains difficult to predict from smaller proxies, even though larger and later proxies become more informative about whether an intervention will matter at the reference scale.


\textbf{Effect of evaluation protocol on DA.}
To examine whether the evaluation protocol affects ranking predictability, Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_bbpb} follows the format of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} but stratifies the DA results by standard benchmark evaluation and benchmark BPB (bBPB). This may indicate that bBPB exposes a smoother likelihood-based signal that makes differences between model variants visible earlier than standard discrete benchmark scoring.

\textbf{Extending the reference to 3B.}
A follow-up question is whether 1.7B is large enough to serve as the reference. We train six of the configurations at 3B (deep, scheme A at $K \in \{8, 15, 30, 50\}$ and scheme B at $K \in \{8, 15\}$), and use these models as the reference (Appendix~\ref{app:rq10_size_generalisation}). The above-chance threshold keeps 778 of 933 benchmark tasks at 3B against 612 at 1.7B, mostly the letter-format originals of Belebele, Global-MMLU and INCLUDE, which their reformulations already make measurable at 1.7B. With respect to ranking agreement, the main conclusions hold. Per-language BPB carries over: the 1.7B models predict the 3B decisions at 0.94--0.95, every proxy from 90M reaches at least 0.78, and the macro average of BPB orders every pair the same way at 1.7B and at 3B. Pooled over decisions, benchmark accuracy reads 0.48--0.53 against either reference on the same decisions, so its gap to BPB does not come from the choice of the smaller 1.7B reference.

\section{RQ3: Anticipation}
\label{sec:rq3}

\begin{rquestion}
\textit{Can cheap proxy statistics predict decision accuracy?}    
\end{rquestion}

%\begin{rqfinding}
%\textbf{1. No single surrogate leads at every proxy size.} Early-checkpoint ranking agreement and the relative-standard-deviation SNR trade the lead, both with a moderate association with DA-size ($\rho \approx 0.6$) that is already present at 90M. \\ \\
%\textbf{2. The predictive part of SNR is its noise term.} Signal alone and the margin above chance carry no information about decision accuracy on benchmark tasks. \\ \\
%\textbf{3. Pooled correlations hide large differences between benchmarks, scorings and languages.} The same SNR tracks decision accuracy closely within HellaSwag but barely on per-language BPB, where the surrogates reverse sign at 600M.
%\end{rqfinding}


As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:rq2}, decision accuracy (DA) measures whether the ranking of models with different development decisions agrees with their ordering at reference scale.
\highlight{However, DA is inherently retrospective, meaning that computing it requires both the proxy ranking and the ranking of the larger reference models.}
Ideally, we would instead like a \highlight{\textit{surrogate metric} that can be computed from the proxy models alone and approximates the DA that would be obtained against the reference.}\clara{would describe this differently. It should be framed more as: are there signals in the evaluations we get from the proxy models as to whether their rankings will be consistent at larger scales? And would also make it explicit that this is a bit of a "meta" metric. Like a metric about whether a metric (DA) will be reliable)}
Prior work has proposed several such diagnostics, most notably signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) \citep{heineman_signal_2025}, which compares the separation between model variants to the variability of their evaluation scores.
These methods have primarily been studied in English settings and at scales where downstream benchmark signal is already measurable.
In this experiment, we investigate first whether existing surrogate metrics remain predictive of DA in the multilingual setting and second, which surrogate provides a reliable signal at the smallest model scale.

We first test whether statistics proposed to characterize evaluation reliability can serve as surrogates for DA without requiring the reference model.
\highlight{For each benchmark--language task and proxy size, we compute each surrogate using only information available at the proxy scale and measure its Spearman ($\rho$) association with DA}, i.e., agreement between the proxy-size ranking and the ranking at the 1.7B reference model.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} shows the Spearman ($\rho$) association with DA of different surrogate implementations across model sizes over all benchmarks that have passed the above-chance threshold, depicting how their predictive strength changes as the proxy scale increases and whether any surrogate remains consistently informative across scales.
We compare several families of surrogate statistics that can be computed without the reference model, including scaling-fit \(R^2\), SNR-based measures \citep{heineman_signal_2025} and their signal/noise components, margin above chance, and early-checkpoint ranking agreement. Scaling-fit \(R^2\) and the margin above chance build on the measurability analysis of \S\ref{sec:rq1}, and early-checkpoint agreement on the training convergence of \S\ref{sec:rq2}.
Scaling-fit \(R^2\) measures how well benchmark performance follows a log-linear trend across the model sizes available up to the proxy point. Margin above chance measures how far the benchmark score is above random-guessing performance. Early-checkpoint ranking agreement measures how well the ranking of model variants at an early training checkpoint matches their ranking at the final checkpoint of the same model size.
Appendix~\ref{app:surrogates} defines the SNR variants and the other surrogates.

\textbf{Surrogate predictivity in the multilingual setting.}
We find that \highlight{no single surrogate consistently predicts decision accuracy} across the multilingual settings of our model ladder.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} shows that among the simple candidates, early-checkpoint ranking agreement and the relative-standard-deviation SNR provide the strongest overall association with DA ($\rho = 0.54$--$0.62$ and $0.53$--$0.60$ across proxy sizes), \textcolor{blue}{suggesting that benchmarks whose ranking settles early in training and whose scores vary little across checkpoints are also more likely to preserve the ranking of model variants across scales.} Intuitively, scaling-fit \(R^2\) follows with $\rho = 0.45$--$0.49$, since benchmarks with more predictable scaling behavior also tend to preserve rankings.
Signal alone ($\rho = -0.16$ to $0.00$) and the margin above chance ($\rho = -0.03$ to $0.09$) carry no positive information about DA. For SNR-based surrogates, however, performance depends strongly on how noise is defined, with some noise estimates yielding substantially stronger associations than others. For example, pooling the proxy sizes with one point per (benchmark, language) pair, replacing checkpoint-based noise with $k$-fold benchmark noise lowers the association of the relative-standard-deviation SNR with DA from \(\rho=0.75\) to \(\rho=0.21\) (Table~\ref{tab:sur-corr-snr}, Appendix~\ref{app:sur-results}).
The aggregate results in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} pool all benchmark--language tasks that pass the chance threshold for all model variant combinations (\ie multi-axis). When we stratify the analysis by benchmark or multilingual training regime, however, stronger relationships emerge. While the strongest association in the pooled benchmark analysis is \(\rho=0.75\), the best SNR definition reaches \(\rho=0.93\) for HellaSwag. 
% also not true anymore that these examples are all higher, numbers fixed: surrogate predictivity rises to \(\rho=0.93\) for HellaSwag, \(\rho=0.70\) for per-language BPB, and \(\rho=0.69\) for models trained on \(8\) languages.These values are \(0.18\) higher, \(0.05\) lower, and \(0.06\) lower than the pooled association, respectively, corresponding to an average increase of \(0.25\) in Spearman correlation.
On the single-axis pairs, the correlations are weaker and the relative-standard-deviation SNR leads at every proxy size ($\rho = 0.38$--$0.46$, against $0.24$--$0.36$ for early-checkpoint agreement, Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq04_surrogates_mono_axis} in Appendix~\ref{app:rq04_surrogates}).
Table~\ref{tab:sur-corr-strata} (Appendix~\ref{app:sur-results}) gives the surrogate-DA Spearman correlations per benchmark, language tier and proxy size, and Appendix~\ref{app:rq04_surrogates} the per-language correlations of every SNR definition.

\textbf{At what model scale does surrogate signal emerge?}
\highlight{We next ask how early in model development a surrogate becomes informative about DA.}
Using the same analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3}, we compare surrogate--DA associations across increasing proxy model sizes, from 90M to 1B.
This allows us to test whether useful surrogate signal is already present at the smallest scale and whether it strengthens or remains stable as model scale increases.
\highlight{At the smallest proxy scale, the two leading surrogates already reach a moderate association with DA at 90M, and neither strengthens consistently as the proxy scales in size.}
Early-checkpoint ranking agreement is strongest at 175M ($\rho=0.62$), and its association stays at $\rho=0.59$ at 350M, indicating that this early signal is fairly consistent across scales.
By 350M, inverted relative-standard-deviation noise reaches $\rho=0.56$ and discrepancy-based SNR reaches $\rho=0.34$.
Scaling-fit $R^2$ becomes available only from 350M onward, where it reaches $\rho=0.49$ and stays at $\rho = 0.45$--$0.49$ up to 1B.
Overall, a moderate surrogate signal is present at the smallest proxy scale but does not strengthen with model size, and the surrogate providing the strongest association is early-checkpoint agreement at every size except 350M, where the relative-standard-deviation SNR leads.
Similar to our earlier findings on multilingual benchmark scaling, we find that decision surrogates behave less consistently than suggested by prior English-focused studies, with their usefulness depending strongly on model scale, benchmark and training-language regime.

\textbf{What a surrogate cannot see.}
Part of this inconsistency has a structural cause. Every surrogate statistic is computed from the proxy runs, so none contains information about the sign of the score difference at the reference size.
The 600M per-language BPB result of \S\ref{sec:rq2} is the measured instance. Per-language BPB scales almost perfectly with model size (median $R^2 = 1.00$, \S\ref{subsec:rq1_scaling}), yet at 600M its DA-size against the 1.7B reference is 0.74, against 0.89--0.94 at the other proxy sizes.
The proxy statistics cannot flag this case, because it comes from the seed draw of the deep 600M runs on pairs whose 1.7B gap is about one seed standard deviation.
At this size the SNR definitions, signal alone and the scaling-fit $R^2$ all turn negative ($\rho = -0.35$ to $-0.60$, Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq04_surrogates_by_scoring}, right), so a statistic that ranks the 600M proxy as reliable points to the wrong decisions.
Two follow-ups would test this limit directly: a held-out-language test of the token thresholds of \S\ref{sec:rq1}, and a separation of within-proxy detectability from cross-scale agreement.
