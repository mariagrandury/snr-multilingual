\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We analyze the task scores $Y_{b_\ell}(\theta,S,f)$ as defined in §\ref{sec:methodology}.
Throughout the analysis, we use these evaluation runs as the basic observations and aggregate them differently depending on the question: across model sizes to study scaling with size, across checkpoints to study training trajectories, and across architectures, mixtures, and seeds to assess robustness.
Using these views, we ask which multilingual measurements track learning, when the rankings they induce transfer to larger models, and whether cheaper statistics can anticipate that transfer.

\subsection{At which size are multilingual benchmark tasks above chance?}
\label{subsec:rq0}

\begin{rqfinding}
1. Most of the evaluation is at chance somewhere on the ladder: of the trained-language benchmark tasks, 37\% are above chance at 90M and 60\% at 1.7B, and 37\% never pass at any size.

2. The answer format, not the number of options, decides who passes: at 1.7B the letter-answer originals of Belebele, Global-MMLU and INCLUDE pass in 5 of 105, 1 of 37 and 3 of 43 language subsets, against 86, 35 and 31 once the options are scored as text, while two- and four-option tasks pass at similar rates.

3. The item count sets the bar: the margin over chance a run needs ranges from $+0.006$ for the largest tasks (14{,}042 items) to $+0.157$ for the smallest (25 items).
\end{rqfinding}


We first ask whether each benchmark provides measurable signal at the model scales considered.
For each benchmark--language task $b_\ell$ and model size $N$, we compare performance against the corresponding chance level, calculated as explained in §\ref{subsec:evaluation}.

\textbf{Effect of task format.}
A remaining concern is that weak scaling may reflect the evaluation protocol rather than the underlying capability measured by the benchmark.
Multiple-choice tasks that present their options as lettered alternatives require the model to map an answer label onto its content, an ability that small models may lack independently of whether they know the answer \citep{khatun_study_2024, madaan_quantifying_2024}.
To isolate this effect, we reformulate the three benchmarks of Belebele, Global-MMLU, and INCLUDE so that the model scores the full answer options directly, rather than predicting their corresponding letter labels. 
We apply two reformulations. The first is programmatic: we remove the answer labels and score the four options directly, leaving the text of every item unchanged. 
The second is model-based: we prompt \texttt{gemini-3.8-flash} \citep{gemini_team_gemini_2024} to restate each question as a declarative stem and each option as a statement completing it, without revealing which option is correct. 
%\AR{Do we have detailed results somewhere? A figure or a table?}
Both reformulations substantially increase the number of benchmark--language subsets that pass the above-chance filter at 1.7B, from 5 to 86 of 105 for Belebele, from 1 to 35 of 37 total language subsets for Global-MMLU, and from 3 to 31 of 43 for INCLUDE. Accuracy on the trained-language subsets rises by 0.098, 0.058, and 0.047, respectively, measured on the same models and checkpoints as the original items. The model-based reformulation, \checknum{which we have so far completed for INCLUDE}, is stronger still: 32 of 43 subsets pass the filter and accuracy rises by 0.075. Gains widen with model size, from 0.015 at 175M to 0.075 at 1.7B, and the rewrite introduces no length cue, as the correct option is the single longest in 22.2\% of original items and 21.9\% of rewritten ones against 25\% by chance. Detailed results by benchmark are in Appendix~\ref{app:chance-threshold}.

\subsection{Which multilingual benchmark tasks scale predictably?}
\label{subsec:rq1}

\begin{rqfinding}
1. Above chance, benchmark accuracy scales log-linearly with model size: the median size-fit $R^2$ is 0.88 over 431 benchmark tasks, and per-language BPB fits at a median $R^2$ of 1.00 over the 50 trained languages.

2. The letter-format knowledge benchmarks have no scaling law on our ladder: Belebele (0 of 59 tasks), INCLUDE (0 of 36) and Global-MMLU (0 of 29) stay at chance wherever a fit is possible, while their reformulated variants fit at a family median $R^2$ of 0.84--0.92.

3. Predictability across size usually comes with predictability across training: 273 of the 352 tasks with a fit (78\%) scale predictably along both axes.
\end{rqfinding}

\textbf{Benchmark scaling behavior.}
For the proxy-reference model pairs above the chance gate, we measure how predictably benchmark performance changes with model size and training progress.
For model size scaling, we fit final-checkpoint performance against $\log_{10}N$ across model sizes, holding model configuration and seed fixed, and require at least three model sizes.
For training scaling, we fit performance against log training tokens within each model, requiring at least five checkpoints.
For each task, we summarize the resulting fits across mixtures that train its language, requiring at least two fits on each axis.
We report $R^2$ as a measure of scaling predictability and Spearman's $\rho$ as a measure of monotonicity and direction.
For BPB and training loss, we reverse the sign of $\rho$ so that positive values consistently indicate improvement.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(a) shows that benchmarks differ substantially in how predictably they scale with model size. HellaSwag, LAMBADA, and BPB have median size-fit $R^2$ values of 0.88, 0.96, and 1.00, respectively, whereas \checknum{Global-MMLU, INCLUDE, and Belebele provide weak fits} on the scaling ladder considered here. The direction of scaling also matters, since high predictability does not necessarily correspond to improvement. For example, TruthfulQA has a median size-fit $R^2$ of 0.97 but $\rho=-0.75$, indicating a strong and consistent decrease with model size and showing why $R^2$ alone is insufficient.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(b) compares scaling predictability across model size and training. Some benchmark evaluations can be well predicted along both dimensions, while others can be predicted across model size but not across training checkpoints. We find no cases where performance is predictable across training checkpoints but not across model size.
This suggests that, for the measurements that exhibit predictable scaling in our setting, varying model size may provide useful signal without requiring long training trajectories.
In other words, in our setting, comparing larger models earlier in training appears more informative than training smaller models for longer.

\subsection{How small and early can a proxy be while preserving the reference decision?}
\label{subsec:rq2}

\begin{rqfinding}
1. A fully trained smaller model ranks the design variants close to a coin flip: on benchmark accuracy, DA-size against 1.7B is 0.51--0.57 from 90M to 1B, and no proxy comes near $\tau = 0.90$.

2. Training a proxy longer does not close the gap: for the 90M and 600M proxies, DA-goal on benchmark accuracy stays at 0.50--0.55 at every evaluated checkpoint.

3. Per-language BPB ranks like the reference where accuracy does not: its DA-size is 0.92--0.96 at every proxy except 600M (0.74), where the depth pairs are not size-matched.
\end{rqfinding}

We next ask how early in model development we can recover the decisions that would be obtained from a full evaluation.
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:methodology}, 
To isolate this question from benchmark quality itself, we restrict the analysis to benchmarks that already exhibit high decision reliability under full evaluation. The selection procedure and per-benchmark results are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}. 
We study decision reliability along two dimensions of model development: training progress and model scale, and analyze it from three complementary perspectives. First, we measure within-model training convergence by comparing the decision at each intermediate checkpoint to the decision made by the final checkpoint of the same model size, testing how early a model’s eventual decision stabilizes during training (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}b). Second, we measure full-scale decision prediction by comparing every intermediate checkpoint, across model sizes, to the fully trained largest model, testing how early smaller and partially trained models can recover the decision of the final reference model (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c). Third, we study scale convergence by comparing the final checkpoint of each model size to the final checkpoint of the largest model, quantifying how small a model can be while still reproducing decisions made at full scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a).

\textbf{Within-model training convergence.}
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}b shows that decision reliability increases steadily over training when each checkpoint is compared to the final checkpoint of the same model. 
Early checkpoints recover only around 61--68\% of the final decisions, while agreement rises substantially later in training, with smaller models generally stabilizing earlier than larger ones.

\textbf{Predicting full-scale decisions from cheaper models.}
Figures~\ref{fig:rq2}a and~\ref{fig:rq2}c show that this within-model convergence does not translate into reliable prediction of the final 1.7B decisions. 
Across fully trained smaller models, agreement with the 1.7B reference remains modest and non-monotonic with scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a), and additional training of smaller models yields only limited improvement in full-scale decision reliability (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c). 
Together, these results suggest that the cross-scale gap cannot be closed by training cheaper proxy models longer alone.


\subsection{Can cheap proxy statistics predict decision accuracy?}
\label{subsec:rq3}

\begin{rqfinding}
1. \checknum{No surrogate leads at every proxy size: early-checkpoint ranking agreement leads at 90M, 175M and 1B ($\rho = 0.59$, $0.62$ and $0.57$ with DA-size), while the relative-standard-deviation SNR leads at 350M and 600M ($\rho = 0.61$ and $0.57$).}

2. \checknum{Early-checkpoint ranking agreement is stable across scale}: $\rho$ stays between 0.55 and 0.62 at every proxy, with 0.59 at 90M, 0.62 at 175M and 0.58 at 350M.

3. Margin above chance and signal alone carry no information on benchmark tasks ($\rho$ between $-0.09$ and $0.11$ at every proxy). The predictive part of SNR is its noise term (0.43--0.56, inverted).
\end{rqfinding}

As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:rq2}, decision accuracy (DA) measures whether the ranking of models with different development decisions agrees with their ordering at reference scale. 
\textcolor{blue}{However, DA is inherently retrospective, meaning that computing it requires both the proxy ranking and the ranking of the larger reference models.}
Ideally, we would instead like a \textcolor{blue}{\textit{surrogate metric} that can be computed from the proxy models alone and approximates the DA that would be obtained against the reference.}
Prior work has proposed several such diagnostics, most notably signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) \citep{heineman_signal_2025}, which compares the separation between model variants to the variability of their evaluation scores.
These methods have primarily been studied in English settings and at scales where downstream benchmark signal is already measurable.
In this experiment, we investigate first whether existing surrogate metrics remain predictive of DA in the multilingual setting and second, which surrogate provides a reliable signal at the smallest model scale.

We first test whether statistics proposed to characterize evaluation reliability can serve as surrogates for DA without requiring the reference model. 
\textcolor{blue}{For each benchmark–language task and proxy size, we compute each surrogate using only information available at the proxy scale and measure its Spearman ($\rho$) association with DA}, i.e., agreement between the proxy-size ranking and the ranking at the 1.7B reference model.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} shows the Spearman ($\rho$) association with DA of different surrogate implementations across model sizes over all benchmarks that have passed the above-chance threshold, depicting how their predictive strength changes as the proxy scale increases and whether any surrogate remains consistently informative across scales. 
We compare several families of surrogate statistics that can be computed without the reference model, including scaling-fit \(R^2\), SNR-based measures \citep{heineman_signal_2025} and their signal/noise components, margin above chance, and early-checkpoint ranking agreement. 
Scaling-fit \(R^2\) measures how well benchmark performance follows a log-linear trend across the model sizes available up to the proxy point; margin above chance measures how far the benchmark score is above random-guessing performance; and early-checkpoint ranking agreement measures how well the ranking of model variants at an early training checkpoint matches their ranking at the final checkpoint of the same model size.
For each benchmark--language task and proxy size, we compute the surrogate using only information available up to that proxy point and measure its Spearman association with DA.
For detailed implementation for the SNR variants, please refer to the Appendix~\ref{app:surrogates}.

\textbf{Surrogate predictivity in the multilingual setting.}
We find that \textcolor{blue}{no single surrogate consistently predicts decision accuracy} across the multilingual settings of our model ladder.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} shows that \checknum{among the simple candidates, early-checkpoint ranking agreement and the relative-standard-deviation SNR provide the strongest overall association with DA}, \textcolor{blue}{suggesting that benchmarks whose ranking settles early in training and whose scores vary little across checkpoints are also more likely to preserve the ranking of model variants across scales.}
For SNR-based surrogates, however, performance depends strongly on how noise is defined, with some noise estimates yielding substantially stronger associations than others. For example, replacing checkpoint-based noise with k-fold benchmark noise lowers the association with DA from \(\rho=0.68\) to \(\rho=0.51\).
The aggregate results in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} pool all benchmark--language tasks that pass the chance threshold. When we stratify the analysis by benchmark or multilingual training regime, however, the relationships differ by stratum. 
While the strongest association in the pooled benchmark analysis is \(\rho=0.79\), surrogate predictivity rises to \(\rho=0.93\) for HellaSwag but reaches only \(\rho=0.62\) for per-language BPB and \(\rho=0.70\) for models trained on \(8\) languages. 
These values are \(0.14\) higher, \(0.17\) lower, and \(0.09\) lower than the pooled association, respectively.
Detailed stratification of surrogate--DA Spearman correlations across benchmarks, numbers of training languages, and model sizes can be found in Appendix~\ref{app:rq04_surrogates}.


\textbf{At what model scale does surrogate signal emerge?}
\textcolor{blue}{We next ask how early in model development a surrogate becomes informative about DA.}
Using the same analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3}, we compare surrogate--DA associations across increasing proxy model sizes, from 90M to 1B.
This allows us to test whether useful surrogate signal is already present at the smallest scale and whether it strengthens or remains stable as model scale increases.
\textcolor{blue}{At the smallest proxy scale, no surrogate provides a strong and stable signal of DA.}
Early-checkpoint ranking agreement is strongest at 175M ($\rho=0.62$), and its association drops only to $\rho=0.58$ at 350M, indicating that this early signal is fairly consistent across scales.
By 350M, inverted relative-standard-deviation noise reaches $\rho=0.56$ and discrepancy-based SNR reaches $\rho=0.33$.
Scaling-fit $R^2$ becomes available only from 350M onward, where it reaches $\rho=0.46$ and is again $\rho=0.46$ at 1B.
Overall, a useful surrogate signal does not emerge reliably at the smallest proxy scale, \checknum{and the surrogate providing the strongest association changes as model size increases}.
Similar to our earlier findings on multilingual benchmark scaling, we find that decision surrogates behave less consistently than suggested by prior English-focused studies, with their usefulness depending strongly on model scale, benchmark and training-language regime.
