\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We organize the analysis around five questions. Which benchmarks scale predictably (\S\ref{subsec:rq1}); how early in scale and in training a design decision can be read (\S\ref{subsec:rq2}); whether a cheap statistic can tell us in advance that the decision will be reliable (\S\ref{subsec:rq3}); whether the answers depend on the design choice being made (\S\ref{subsec:rq4}); and whether they transfer to languages we never measured (\S\ref{subsec:rq5}). Each subsection opens with the finding, then states the setup and the evidence.

\textbf{What the ladder contains.} The analysis reads 98 healthy cells: 24 at 175M, 18 at 350M, 24 at 600M, 19 at 1B and 12 at 1.7B, plus the one 90M run that did not diverge. Every cell is scored on all 100 validation languages. A design decision is read against the largest size trained at both of its levels with a final-checkpoint score, which is 1.7B for the language-list decision at $L \in \{8, 30\}$ and 1B at $L=15$, and 600M for the depth decision at $L \geq 2$, because the shallow 1.7B cells have not yet been scored on bits per byte at their final checkpoint. Every value below is reported with the reference it resolves against.

\textbf{The above-random gate.} A (benchmark, size) cell enters a benchmark analysis only if its mean score beats chance by 0.05, with chance taken from the task's option count. Of the 462 tasks that have a chance level, 114 clear it at some size and 348 never do. The gate opens with size: 3 tasks clear it at 90M, 62 at 175M, 72 at 350M, 87 at 600M, 99 at 1B and 112 at 1.7B. Bits per byte has no chance level and is never gated.

\subsection{RQ1: What scales predictably?}
\label{subsec:rq1}

\textbf{TL;DR.} Completion-format benchmarks and per-language bits per byte follow a log-linear trend in model size almost exactly, with median $R^2$ above 0.9. Four-option knowledge benchmarks do not move with size at all: Global-MMLU, INCLUDE and Belebele have median $R^2$ below 0.3 and are the families the gate removes. The answer count, not the construction, separates the two groups.

\textbf{Setup.} For every task and language setting we fit the final-checkpoint score of the deep, scheme-A, seed-1904 ladder against $\log_{10} N$ over the rungs from 175M to 1.7B, and record the coefficient of determination $R^2$ and the Spearman correlation $\rho$ between score and size. A fit needs at least three rungs. Bits per byte and the training loss fall as models grow, so $\rho = -1$ is their ideal. We report medians over each family's (task, $L$) fits: 3,408 fits over 568 tasks, of which 2,802 are benchmark fits. The 90M rung is excluded (\S\ref{subsec:models}).

(a) Accuracy above chance against non-embedding parameters at $L=30$, one faint line per language task and a bold line per family median, for two families that scale (MultiBLiMP, HellaSwag), one that starts to (XNLI), and two that stay at chance up to 1.7B (Belebele, Global-MMLU). (b) Median $R^2$ of the log-linear fit and median Spearman $\rho$ against size per family, with the number of (task, $L$) fits and the answer count. \textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:rq1} shows a clean split. Multilingual LAMBADA ($R^2 = 0.99$), per-language bits per byte (0.95), XStoryCloze (0.94), XWinograd (0.94), the training loss (0.94) and HellaSwag (0.91) are near-perfectly ordered by size, and MultiBLiMP (0.85, $\rho = 0.97$ over 342 fits) and XNLI (0.81) follow. At the other end, Global-MMLU ($R^2 = 0.22$, $\rho = -0.2$), INCLUDE (0.25, $\rho = 0.0$) and Belebele (0.29) have scores that are close to unrelated to size, and Global PIQA sits in between (0.34). The split follows the answer space: the median $R^2$ is 0.64 over the 1,152 two-option fits and 0.81 over the 108 three-option ones, against 0.39 over the 1,512 four-option fits. Panel (a) shows why. At $L = 30$ the four-option knowledge families never leave the chance line up to 1.7B, so there is no trend to fit, while MultiBLiMP is 0.24 above chance already at 175M. TruthfulQA is the one family with a tight fit and a negative slope ($R^2 = 0.68$, $\rho = -0.57$): it reliably gets worse as models grow, a known inverse-scaling behaviour. This indicates that at these sizes the predictable multilingual measurements are the likelihood-based ones, and that a knowledge benchmark that sits at chance contributes no trend to extrapolate from, whatever its provenance.

\subsection{RQ2: How early can we make the right decision?}
\label{subsec:rq2}

\textbf{TL;DR.} On per-language bits per byte, a 350M proxy reads the language-list decision the way the 1.7B reference does at 8, 15 and 30 languages, and the reference's own ranking is fixed by 20\% of its run. The 175M proxy gets the decision backwards at 15 and 30 languages. On the benchmark suite the decision is a coin flip at every size and every checkpoint.

\textbf{Setup.} For an intervention with two levels, decision accuracy reduces to sign agreement: over a population of items, the share on which the proxy prefers the same level as the reference. The reference is the largest size trained at both levels, read at its final checkpoint. The proxy is every smaller size, read at the checkpoint nearest 20, 40, 60, 80 and 100\% of its own run, so that one grid answers both halves of the question, how small and how early. Two populations are used: the per-language bits per byte of the languages both levels train on, and the benchmark tasks both levels were evaluated on. A cell needs at least three items. We report the mean over language settings of the per-setting agreement, so that a setting with 2,250 benchmark tasks does not outweigh one with 500. Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} shows the two planned decisions, language lists (scheme A against B at $L \in \{8, 15, 30\}$) and depth (deep against shallow at every $L$).

\caption{How small and how early a proxy can read a decision. Each cell is the agreement between the proxy's decision, at the given size and fraction of its own run, and the reference's final decision, averaged over language settings (number of settings in brackets). Rows: the two planned decisions. Columns: per-language bits per byte over the languages both levels train, and the benchmark tasks both levels were evaluated on. The reference is 1.7B at $L \in \{8, 30\}$ and 1B at $L = 15$ for the language-list decision, and 600M for the depth decision at $L \geq 2$; the bottom row of each panel is the reference read at its own earlier checkpoints.}
\label{fig:rq2}

\textbf{Results.} On bits per byte the language-list decision has a clean size threshold. The 350M proxy agrees with the reference on 100\% of the shared languages at $L = 8$, 15 and 30, and the 600M proxy on 100, 83 and 100\%. The 175M proxy agrees at $L = 8$ but gets the decision backwards at $L = 15$ (17\%) and $L = 30$ (4\%). The 1B proxy, which exists only against the 1.7B reference at $L = 8$ and 30, agrees on 100\% at $L = 8$ but only 31\% at $L = 30$. The $L = 30$ discrepancy has a reason: the effect of the list decision on bits per byte at the 1.7B reference is 0.58 seed standard deviations there (\S\ref{subsec:rq4}), so the reference itself is deciding inside its noise and every proxy's agreement at that setting is uninformative. Where the effect clears the noise, at $L = 8$ (1.6 seed standard deviations) and $L = 15$ (2.7), 350M is enough.

The training axis is even more favourable. The reference's ranking at 20\% of its run already matches its final ranking on 100\% of the languages for the list decision and on 100\% for the depth decision, and stays there through 80\%. Four fifths of the reference run adds nothing to the decision. The one exception is the smallest proxy, which reverses its answer over its own run: at 175M the list decision agrees with the reference on 86\% of languages at 20\% of training and on 40\% at the end, so the 175M final checkpoint is a worse proxy than its own early checkpoint. We hypothesize that this is the optimizer-timescale effect of \S\ref{subsec:models} reaching the 175M rung, whose run is shorter than the optimizer's memory.

The depth decision behaves differently, and \S\ref{subsec:rq4} explains why: its effect at the reference is within 1.1 to 2.4 seed standard deviations, so the 175M proxy's 0\% agreement and the 350M proxy's 51\% are agreement with a decision that the reference is not really making. The benchmark suite reads every cell of both decisions as a coin flip: between 0.37 and 0.58 at every size and every fraction of training, including the reference read against its own final checkpoint. This suggests that the suite's tasks do not carry the decision at all rather than carrying it late, which \S\ref{subsec:rq3} confirms from the other side.

\subsection{RQ3: Can we know cheaply whether the decision will be reliable?}
\label{subsec:rq3}

\textbf{TL;DR.} Partly. The signal-to-noise ratio computed on the proxy alone, in its relative-standard-deviation form, ranks tasks by their eventual decision accuracy with a Spearman correlation of 0.4 to 0.6 on benchmarks and 0.5 to 0.7 on per-language bits per byte, and the agreement between a proxy's ranking at 20\% of its run and at its end does about as well. The signal term carries the correlation; noise on its own predicts nothing, and the discrepancy variant is uninformative on this ladder.

\textbf{Setup.} Decision accuracy needs the reference model. We ask which statistics that are available on the proxy alone predict it. For every task and proxy size we compute the signal-to-noise ratio under three of the definitions of \S\ref{subsec:methodology-snr} (relative standard deviation, discrepancy, and the standard deviation of pairwise distances), the signal and the noise on their own, the margin above chance, the coefficient of determination of the scaling fit of \S\ref{subsec:rq1}, and the agreement between the proxy's ranking at 20\% of its run and at its end. Each candidate is correlated (Spearman $\rho$) with the task's decision accuracy by size, the agreement between the ranking of all design variants at the proxy size and at the reference, computed by the pipeline of \citet{heineman_signal_2025} on the same cells. We report the correlation separately for benchmark tasks and for the per-language bits-per-byte tasks, at the 175M, 350M and 600M proxies.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rq3_surrogates.pdf}
\caption{Which small-scale statistic predicts decision accuracy. Spearman correlation between each candidate, computed on the proxy alone, and the task's decision accuracy against the reference, for benchmark tasks (left) and per-language bits per byte (right), at three proxy sizes; $n$ is the number of tasks behind each point.}
\label{fig:rq3}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} scores every candidate on the same population, the tasks that clear the gate at the proxy size: 68, 78 and 93 benchmark tasks at 175M, 350M and 600M, and the 101 bits-per-byte tasks. Decision accuracy against the 1B target is 0.73, 0.71 and 0.79 (median) for those benchmark tasks and 0.81, 0.89 and 0.89 for bits per byte, so there is variation to predict on both sides. The relative-standard-deviation SNR is the strongest or joint-strongest predictor at every proxy: $\rho = 0.52$, 0.60 and 0.41 on benchmarks and 0.69, 0.60 and 0.52 on bits per byte. Its signal term alone is almost as good (0.48, 0.54, 0.36 and 0.62, 0.50, 0.48), while its noise term alone is at most 0.23 anywhere, which indicates that on this ladder the SNR works because of the dispersion across design variants, not because of the checkpoint noise. The discrepancy variant is uninformative on benchmarks (0.11, 0.16, $-0.01$) and undefined on bits per byte, and the margin above chance predicts little (0.27 at 175M, negative at 600M): clearing chance is a precondition, not a ranking.

The cheapest surrogate is the proxy's own training curve. The agreement between a proxy's ranking of the variants at 20\% of its run and at its end correlates with its eventual decision accuracy at 0.33, 0.59 and 0.57 on benchmarks and 0.61, 0.62 and 0.55 on bits per byte, matching the SNR from 350M up without any noise estimate. The coefficient of determination of the scaling fit is a weak predictor on bits per byte (0.10 to 0.22), where every language scales predictably, and a moderate one on benchmarks (0.21 to 0.48), where it separates the families of \S\ref{subsec:rq1}. This suggests a two-step rule for practitioners: read the decision on bits per byte, and check on the proxy alone that the variants are already ordered the same way at a fifth of the run and that their dispersion exceeds the late-checkpoint noise. Both statistics are available before the reference exists.

\subsection{RQ4: Does it depend on the design choice?}
\label{subsec:rq4}

\textbf{TL;DR.} Yes, through the size of the effect. The two data decisions that change the training distribution most, sampling temperature and the choice of second language, move bits per byte at the reference by 3.4 to 4.9 seed standard deviations and are read correctly by a 350M proxy. The depth and language-list decisions move it by 1.5 to 1.6, and a proxy can only be as reliable as the reference's own coin. Benchmarks sit at 1.1 to 1.8 seed standard deviations for every decision and read every one of them as a coin flip.

\textbf{Setup.} Before asking whether a proxy agrees with the reference, we ask whether the reference is deciding anything. For each intervention and language setting we measure the absolute difference between the two levels at the reference, per item, in units of that item's seed standard deviation, taken as the median over the seed-replicated cells of the baseline (deep, scheme A) at the same task. A ratio near 1 means the two levels are the same model as far as a ranking is concerned. We then read the decision accuracy of each proxy size at its final checkpoint, as in \S\ref{subsec:rq2}, for all five interventions the ladder trains: depth, language lists, sampling temperature ($T = 1$ against $T = 3$ at $L = 50$), and the second language at $L = 2$ (Russian against Chinese, and against Spanish). The second-language decision has one shared trained language, English, so its bits-per-byte population is the 98 languages neither level trains on.

(a) Median absolute effect of each intervention at the reference, in seed standard deviations, on per-language bits per byte and on benchmark tasks; the dashed line is one seed re-roll. (b) Agreement of each proxy size with the reference's final decision, averaged over language settings, on bits per byte (solid) and on benchmarks (dashed). \textbf{Results.} Figure~\ref{fig:rq4}a orders the interventions by how much they matter. On bits per byte, the median effect at the reference is 4.9 seed standard deviations for Russian against Chinese, 3.9 for sampling temperature and 3.4 for Russian against Spanish, against 1.6 for the language lists and 1.5 for depth. On benchmarks every intervention sits between 1.1 and 1.8. Figure~\ref{fig:rq4}b shows that the proxy's reliability follows the same order. The temperature decision is read correctly on 88\% of the trained languages by 175M and 96\% by 350M, against a 600M reference; the second-language decisions on 75 to 78\% at 175M and 86 to 90\% at 350M over the never-trained languages. The list decision needs 350M (\S\ref{subsec:rq2}), and the depth decision is not recovered by any proxy. Its effect is the smallest of the five, which is what the shallow ladder was designed for: the same layer structure at roughly twice the aspect ratio, near the optimum. On the aggregate training loss the two architectures differ by about one seed standard deviation, so the depth "decision" at the reference is largely a re-roll of the seed, and the 0\% agreement of 175M reflects the shallow 175M run being depressed rather than a wrong ranking of a real effect.

The benchmark suite is flat across interventions: between 0.30 and 0.54 at every proxy size for every decision, with no gain from a larger proxy. This indicates that benchmark predictivity here is not conditional on the design choice. It is absent for all of them, because at these sizes the tasks that survive the gate separate the intervention levels by about one checkpoint's worth of noise. We hypothesize that the property that transfers across interventions is the effect-to-noise ratio of the measurement, which bits per byte inherits from being byte-normalised and averaged over millions of tokens, and which a 500-item accuracy does not.

\subsection{RQ5: Does it generalize to languages we have not measured?}
\label{subsec:rq5}

\textbf{TL;DR.} The scaling exponent transfers across languages. Fitting nothing on the held-out language beyond a single 175M measurement, and borrowing the exponent from the other languages, predicts its bits per byte at the 1.7B reference within 3.4\% for trained languages and 6.7\% for languages the model never saw. That beats a per-language fit on three rungs. The list decision also transfers to never-trained languages, though less cleanly: 76\% agreement at 600M against 94\% on the trained ones.

\textbf{Setup.} Bits per byte is scored on all 100 validation languages for every cell, so 45 of them are measured on models that never trained on them. On the deep, scheme-A ladder at each $L$ we fit, per language, $\log \mathrm{BPB} = a_\ell - \alpha_\ell \log N$ on the proxy rungs. We then hold each language out in turn and predict its reference bits per byte with the median exponent of the other 99 languages at that $L$ and an intercept fitted on its own $k$ smallest rungs, for $k$ from one (175M alone) to four. We compare the relative error at the reference against the language's own fit (which needs three rungs) and against taking the largest proxy's value as the prediction. The reference is 1.7B at $L \in \{1, 2, 8, 30\}$ and 1B at $L \in \{15, 50\}$; 106 (language, $L$) chains are trained languages and 494 are not. For decisions, we read the language-list decision of \S\ref{subsec:rq2} on the languages neither scheme trains.

\begin{figure}[t]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rq5_transfer.pdf}
\caption{Transfer of scaling behaviour to held-out languages. (a) Median absolute relative error of the reference's bits per byte as a function of how many of the held-out language's smallest rungs are measured, with the exponent borrowed from the other languages (solid), fitted on the language itself (dashed, needs three rungs), or with no extrapolation at all (dotted), for trained and never-trained languages. (b) Predicted against observed reference bits per byte from a single 175M measurement per language.}
\label{fig:rq5}
\end{figure}

\textbf{Results.} The exponent barely varies between languages: its median is 0.14 at $L = 1$, 0.16 to 0.17 at $L \in \{2, 8, 15, 30\}$ and 0.20 at $L = 50$, with a standard deviation across languages of 0.03 to 0.07. Consequently, the borrowed exponent with a single 175M measurement predicts the reference within a median 3.4\% for trained languages and 6.7\% for never-trained ones (Figure~\ref{fig:rq5}a), and 71\% of the never-trained predictions land within 10\%. Adding rungs helps only the trained languages, to 2.1\% at three rungs. Notably, the borrowed exponent beats the language's own fit at every $k$ where both exist, 2.1\% against 5.1\% at three rungs, because a three-point power law fitted to one language overfits its noise while the pooled exponent does not. The no-extrapolation baseline is 36 to 45\% off from one rung and still 6.5 to 7.3\% off from four. Never-trained languages are predicted with a small systematic bias (median signed error $-3.4$\%), so the reference is slightly better on them than the proxies imply: zero-shot transfer improves with size faster than the trained-language exponent predicts. Panel (b) shows the two populations on one line across a five-fold range of bits per byte.

The decision reads transfer less cleanly. On the languages neither scheme trains, the list decision is read correctly by 56\% at 175M, 68\% at 350M and 76\% at 600M and 1B, against 40, 100, 94 and 65\% on the trained languages. This suggests that a proxy's decision on the languages it was trained on is more reliable than on the rest, but that the direction of a data decision does carry over to unseen languages at 600M and above. We leave the question of which language properties predict the residual to future work.
