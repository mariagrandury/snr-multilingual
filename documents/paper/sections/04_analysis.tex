\section{Analysis}
\label{sec:results}

We analyze the task scores $Y_{b_\ell}(\theta,S,f)$ as defined in \S\ref{sec:methodology}.
Throughout the analysis, we use these evaluation runs as the basic observations and aggregate them differently depending on the question: across model sizes to study scaling with size, across checkpoints to study training trajectories, and across architectures, mixtures, and seeds to assess robustness.
% We organize the analysis as three questions, in the order a developer asks them. \S\ref{subsec:rq0}: at which per-language training-token counts does an evaluation produce a score distinguishable from its own noise? \S\ref{subsec:rq2}: among the evaluations that pass this test, at which proxy sizes and training fractions does the ranking of model variants at the proxy point agree with their ranking at the reference point? \S\ref{subsec:rq3}: which statistics computed from the proxy runs alone predict that agreement?
We organize the analysis around three components. \S\ref{subsec:rq0} examines when evaluation scores become reliable as training increases, \S\ref{subsec:rq2} measures when smaller or earlier evaluations recover the final ranking of model variants, and \S\ref{subsec:rq3} investigates which proxy statistics predict this ranking agreement.

\subsection{Measurability: At which size and token counts are multilingual benchmark tasks above chance?}
\label{subsec:rq0}

\begin{rqfinding}
(These blocks are for internal alignment, we will remove them before
submission)
\\ \\
\textbf{1. Much of the multilingual evaluation is at chance on our ladder.} Of the benchmark tasks in trained languages, only 37\% are above chance at 90M and still only 60\% at 1.7B.
\\
\\
\textbf{2. The answer format, not the language, decides which tasks pass.} Scoring the answer text instead of a letter lifts Belebele from 5 to 86 of its 105 languages at 1.7B, while the letter-format INCLUDE stays mostly at chance however many tokens of the language the model has seen.
\\
\\
\textbf{3. Above chance, benchmark accuracy scales predictably with model size.} A log-linear fit in model size explains most of the variance of a typical task ($R^2 = 0.88$), and the tasks that scale predictably with size usually also do so along training.
\end{rqfinding}

We first ask whether each benchmark provides measurable signal at the model scales considered.
For each benchmark-language task $b_\ell$ and model size $N$, we compare performance against the corresponding chance level, calculated as explained in \S\ref{subsec:evaluation}.

\textbf{Above chance per size.} 
Of the trained-language benchmark tasks, 37\% are above chance at 90M and 60\% at 1.7B, and 37\% never pass at any size (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_gate_and_curves}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_gate_and_curves}). The full grid per benchmark and language is in Figure~\ref{fig:app_chance_full}. We propose the bBPB of a task (\S\ref{subsec:evaluation}), which is read on the same items, as a substitute measurement where accuracy does not pass the above-chance threshold (\S\ref{subsec:rq2}, Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_bbpb} in Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}).

\textbf{Effect of answer format.}
A remaining concern is that weak scaling may reflect the evaluation protocol rather than the underlying capability measured by the benchmark.
Multiple-choice tasks that present their options as lettered alternatives require the model to map an answer label onto its content, an ability that small models may lack independently of whether they know the answer \citeneeded. %\citep{khatun_study_2024, madaan_quantifying_2024}.
To isolate this effect, we reformulate the three benchmarks of Belebele, Global-MMLU, and INCLUDE so that the model scores the full answer options directly, rather than predicting their corresponding letter labels.
We apply two reformulations. The first is programmatic: we remove the answer labels and score the four options directly, leaving the text of every item unchanged.
The second is model-based: we prompt \texttt{gemini-3.8-flash} \citep{gemini_team_gemini_2024} to restate each question as a declarative stem and each option as a statement completing it, without revealing which option is correct.
Both reformulations substantially increase the number of benchmark-language subsets that pass the above-chance filter at 1.7B. The programmatic one raises it from 5 to 86 of 105 for Belebele, from 1 to 35 of 37 total language subsets for Global-MMLU, and from 3 to 31 of 43 for INCLUDE. Accuracy on the trained-language subsets rises by 0.098, 0.058, and 0.047, respectively, measured on the same models and checkpoints as the original items. The model-based reformulation is stronger still: 32 of 43 INCLUDE subsets pass the filter and accuracy rises by 0.075, and on Belebele every trained-language subset passes at every size and accuracy rises by 0.168. On INCLUDE its gains widen with model size, from 0.015 at 175M to 0.075 at 1.7B, and the rewrite introduces no length cue, as the correct option is the single longest in 22.2\% of original items and 21.9\% of rewritten ones against 25\% by chance. Detailed results by benchmark are in Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq00_task_reformulation} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_task_reformulation}) and Figure~\ref{fig:app_chance_reformulation} (Appendix~\ref{app:chance-threshold}).
These above random gate results suggest that much of the weak scaling in these benchmarks comes from the answer format rather than the capability being tested.
Once models score the answer text directly, the same items provide substantially clearer signal across the scaling ladder, showing that benchmark predictability and usefulness for scaling predictions depend also on how it is evaluated.

\textbf{Above chance per training tokens.}
The share increases with the token count of the language. Pooled over all benchmarks outside English, it is flat at about 40\% up to $10^{8}$ tokens and rises with every decade after, to 62\% at $10^{10}$--$10^{11}$ tokens.
The curves of the six model sizes do not coincide: at $10^{8}$--$10^{9}$ tokens, 40\% of the 90M cells are above chance against 60\% of the 1.7B cells, so exposure to the language is necessary but not sufficient, and the token count a family needs depends on the model size (Table~\ref{tab:rq0_thresholds}).
The original letter-format tasks fail the filter at nearly every token count reached in the sweep: the original INCLUDE stays at 5--11\% of its cells from $10^{7}$ tokens on, while its reformulated version reaches 99\% (Figure~\ref{fig:rq0}).
A small set of tasks passes at very small token counts and fails at intermediate ones. Two candidate causes are a preference for the longest option at early checkpoints %(testable from the stored per-option outputs)
and training tokens of related languages.

\textbf{Token thresholds per benchmark.}
Table~\ref{tab:rq0_thresholds} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq00_chance_vs_train_tokens}) gives, per benchmark and answer format, the training-token count of the language from which at least 50\% and 90\% of the cells pass the above-chance filter. Nine of the 27 benchmark formats (21 originals and 6 reformulated versions) never reach 50\%, among them the letter-format originals of Belebele, Global-MMLU and INCLUDE, whose reformulated versions reach it at $2.2\times10^{6}$ to $4.6\times10^{8}$ tokens. The threshold is not set by the item count alone: an original and its reformulated version share their items and their margin, yet only the reformulated one passes. It also moves with model size, by up to two orders of magnitude at the 90\% level.

\textbf{Item-count dependence of the filter.} The margin a task needs above chance shrinks with its item count. The median required margins are 9.00 percentage points for nonparallel Global PIQA at 100 items per language, 2.44 for Belebele at 900, 0.74 for HellaSwag at about 9{,}000 and 0.60 for Global-MMLU at about 14{,}000. Over the six sizes from 90M to 1.7B, these families pass the filter in 37.5\%, 1.1\%, 100.0\% and 4.1\% of their cells, and the reformulated Belebele and Global-MMLU in 75.4\% and 73.9\% (Appendix~\ref{app:chance-threshold}). A family with few items is reported as unable to detect small departures from chance, not as unmeasurable.

\textbf{Benchmark scaling behavior.}
\label{subsec:rq1} % label kept here so existing \ref{subsec:rq1} citations resolve
For the proxy-reference model pairs above the chance gate, we measure how predictably benchmark performance changes with model size and training progress.
Measurements without a chance level, per-language bits per byte in particular, are not gated, so for them this test of consistent change with model size and training tokens is the only check of measurability.
For model size scaling, we fit final-checkpoint performance against $\log_{10}N$ across model sizes, holding model configuration and seed fixed, and require at least three model sizes.
For training scaling, we fit performance against log training tokens within each model, requiring at least five checkpoints.
For each task, we summarize the resulting fits across mixtures that train its language, requiring at least two fits on each axis.
We report $R^2$ as a measure of scaling predictability and Spearman's $\rho$ as a measure of monotonicity and direction.
For BPB and training loss, we reverse the sign of $\rho$ so that positive values consistently indicate improvement.

Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(a) shows that benchmarks differ substantially in how predictably they scale with model size. Above chance, the median size-fit $R^2$ is 0.88 over 431 benchmark tasks and 1.00 for the per-language BPB of the 50 trained languages (Appendix~\ref{app:rq01_scaling_predictability}). HellaSwag, LAMBADA, and BPB have median size-fit $R^2$ values of 0.88, 0.96, and 1.00, respectively, whereas the letter-format Global-MMLU, INCLUDE, and Belebele have no fit at all on the scaling ladder considered here, because they stay at chance wherever a fit is possible. Their reformulated versions fit at family medians of 0.84--0.92 (Appendix~\ref{app:rq01_scaling_predictability}, with the curves in Figures~\ref{fig:app_size_scaling} and~\ref{fig:app_training_scaling}). The direction of scaling also matters, since high predictability does not necessarily correspond to improvement. For example, TruthfulQA has a median size-fit $R^2$ of 0.97 but $\rho=-0.75$, indicating a strong and consistent decrease with model size and showing why $R^2$ alone is insufficient.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}(b) compares scaling predictability across model size and training. Some benchmark evaluations can be well predicted along both dimensions, while others can be predicted across model size but not across training checkpoints. Of the 352 accuracy and BPB tasks with a fit, 273 (78\%) are predictable along both. We find no accuracy or BPB task that is predictable across training checkpoints but not across model size, and only 9 of the 609 bBPB tasks are.
This suggests that, for the measurements that exhibit predictable scaling in our setting, varying model size may provide useful signal without requiring long training trajectories.
In other words, in our setting, comparing larger models earlier in training appears more informative than training smaller models for longer.

\textbf{Monolingual comparison.} We repeat the analysis on the monolingual English cells ($K = 1$), under the same filter and fits. For each of the three English corpora (the DCLM-Edu English of every other cell, DCLM without the educational filter, and FineWeb), 48\% of the 33 English accuracy tasks are predictable along both size and training (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq13_english_scaling_regimes_l1_accuracy}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq13_english_only}). These single-cell fits are not comparable in level with the medians over $K$ of Figure~\ref{fig:rq1}.

A (task, language) cell whose training-token count of the language is below the family's threshold is excluded from development decisions and from aggregate scores. % TODO: supporting analysis from stored outputs: the standard error (the spread of the per-language values divided by the square root of the language count) and the decision accuracy of an aggregate ranking, computed with and without the excluded cells.

\subsection{Agreement: How small and early can a proxy be while preserving the reference decision?}
\label{subsec:rq2}

\begin{rqfinding}
\textbf{1. A fully trained smaller model ranks the design variants close to a coin flip on benchmark accuracy.} Against the 1.7B reference it agrees on only 51--57\% of the decisions, from 90M to 1B.
\\
\\
\textbf{2. Training a smaller proxy longer does not close the gap.} Its ranking converges to its own final ranking during training, but its agreement with the 1.7B ranking stays flat at every checkpoint.
\\
\\
\textbf{3. Per-language BPB ranks like the reference where accuracy does not.} It recovers more than 90\% of the reference decisions from every proxy size except 600M.
\end{rqfinding}

We next ask how early in model development we can recover the decisions that would be obtained from a full evaluation.
We measure decision accuracy (Eq.~\ref{eq:decision-accuracy}) in the three ranking-agreement settings of Table~\ref{tab:ra_defs}: DA-size for RA-size, DA-ckpt for RA-checkpoint and DA-goal for RA-end-to-end.
The pairs are formed among the seed-1904 runs of every language setting, ladder and data build, from 90M to 1.7B, and the reference is the final checkpoint of the 1.7B run of each configuration.
The analysis reads only the cells retained by the above-chance filter of \S\ref{subsec:rq0}, at the proxy and at the reference, together with the bBPB of every benchmark task, which has no chance level.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} reads the single-axis pairs and further keeps the tasks whose median DA-size or median DA-ckpt is at least 0.66 (\S\ref{subsec:methodology-da}). This filter selects on decision accuracy, so the figure is conditional on it. The unfiltered population, every gated task and every pair, is in Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_decision_accuracy} (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}), and choosing the reliable tasks out of sample moves DA-size by at most 0.02 (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_rq2_crossfit}).

We study decision reliability along two dimensions of model development: training progress and model scale, and analyze it from three complementary perspectives. First, we measure within-model training convergence by comparing the decision at each intermediate checkpoint to the decision made by the final checkpoint of the same model size, testing how early a model's eventual decision stabilizes during training (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}b). Second, we measure full-scale decision prediction by comparing every intermediate checkpoint, across model sizes, to the fully trained largest model, testing how early smaller and partially trained models can recover the decision of the final reference model (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c). Third, we study scale convergence by comparing the final checkpoint of each model size to the final checkpoint of the largest model, quantifying how small a model can be while still reproducing decisions made at full scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a).

\textbf{Within-model training convergence.}
Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}b shows that decision reliability increases steadily over training when each checkpoint is compared to the final checkpoint of the same model.
Early checkpoints recover only around 61--68\% of the final decisions, while agreement rises substantially later in training, to 84--92\% at 4.5C, with smaller models generally stabilizing earlier than larger ones.
Part of this rise is persistence within a run rather than a decision. Two seeds of one configuration, which have nothing to decide, already agree on 78--80\% of the pairs at 90\% of training, against 82--86\% for real design pairs (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_seed_uncertainty}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}).

\textbf{Predicting full-scale decisions from cheaper models.}
Figures~\ref{fig:rq2}a and~\ref{fig:rq2}c show that this within-model convergence does not translate into reliable prediction of the final 1.7B decisions.
Across fully trained smaller models, agreement with the 1.7B reference remains modest and non-monotonic with scale (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a), and additional training of smaller models yields only limited improvement in full-scale decision reliability (Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}c).
Without the reliability filter, on benchmark accuracy alone, DA-size is only 0.51--0.57 from 90M to 1B, while the per-language BPB of the 50 trained languages reaches 0.92--0.96 at every proxy except 600M, where it drops to 0.74 (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_decision_accuracy}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}). At 600M the depth pairs are not size-matched: the shallow 600M model has 3.7\% more non-embedding parameters than the deep one and spends about 15\% more training compute (Table~\ref{tab:ladder}).
The 600M drop comes almost entirely from these pairs (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq03_flip_attribution}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq03_noise_and_snr}). On single-axis pairs, per-language BPB at 600M orders the depth decision against the 1.7B reference in 95\% of the (pair, language) comparisons, against at most 16\% for the language count, the data scheme and the temperature. Most of these depth comparisons differ at 600M by less than one seed standard deviation, so the 600M comparison cannot distinguish the two depths rather than measuring an opposite effect. The deep 600M run of seed 1904 is also the least favourable of its three seeds: with the other two seeds, the same depth pair flips on 15 and 43 of the 50 languages instead of all 50.
Together, these results suggest that the cross-scale gap cannot be closed by training cheaper proxy models longer alone.


\textbf{Sensitivity analysis.}
These conclusions do not depend on how DA is computed (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq02_definition_sensitivity}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}).
Counting a tied pair as one half raises benchmark-accuracy DA-size by about 0.03, and keeping only the pairs whose 1.7B difference exceeds two checkpoint standard deviations of the reference raises it to 0.56--0.63 while dropping half of the pairs. Per-language BPB never ties, and the same filter lifts it to 0.99--1.00 at every proxy except 600M (0.88).
Chance agreement is measured cell by cell by shuffling the proxy scores: it sits at 0.49 for the benchmark tasks and their bBPB twins, so their observed DA-size of 0.58--0.59 is only 0.08--0.10 above it, and only 8--10\% of the benchmark cells beat their own null, while per-language BPB reads 0.79--0.95 against a null of 0.50 (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_permutation_null}).
Restricting to the pairs that the 1.7B reference separates by more than one or two seed standard deviations raises DA-size on the benchmark tasks and their bBPB twins to 0.64--0.66 and 0.71--0.73, but these pairs are only 52\% and 25\% of the comparable ones (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decisive_pairs}).
The reliability filter of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2} raises the level of the lines, but its selection effect is small: chosen out of sample, the reliable tasks move DA-size by at most 0.02 (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_rq2_crossfit}), and holding the task set fixed leaves the lines unchanged (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_fixed_rq2}, Appendix~\ref{app:fixed_populations}).


\textbf{Effect of evaluation protocol on DA.}
How a benchmark is scored changes how its decisions transfer across sizes (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_bbpb}). On the reliable tasks of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}, accuracy-based DA-size is higher at larger proxies, from 0.57 at 90M to 0.69 at 1B, while bBPB stays at 0.60--0.65 at every size, so the flat line of Figure~\ref{fig:rq2}a is mostly that of the bBPB twins.
Over every gated task, the bBPB of a proxy predicts the 1.7B accuracy ranking at 0.58--0.60 from any proxy size, against 0.51--0.57 for accuracy itself, a gain of 0.12 at 90M and 0.03 at 1B (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}).
On the tasks where accuracy is still at chance at the proxy, bBPB ranks the design variants like the 1.7B bBPB at 0.61--0.64, above accuracy on the same tasks (0.46--0.50) but far below per-language BPB (Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_decision_accuracy}). Scoring these items by log-likelihood therefore recovers a ranking above chance, not one as reliable as BPB.

\textbf{Per-language BPB and the tokens of the language.}
Per-language BPB does not need the token thresholds of \S\ref{subsec:rq0} to agree with the reference. The 90M proxies already recover 95\% of the 1.7B decisions at the first tenth of their run, after about $8\times10^{6}$ tokens of a language, and 175M recovers 96\% at its final checkpoint. The 600M proxies stay at 70--74\% over their whole run, while 350M and 1B climb to 92\% (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_da_vs_train_tokens}, Appendix~\ref{app:rq02_da_vs_train_tokens}).

\textbf{Reference choice.}
The 1.7B reference is a single run per configuration, so measured DA compares the proxy ranking to one realization of the reference ranking. The decisive-pair filter above bounds this effect from one side. At 1B, the only size above 600M with three seeds, two seeds of the same English designs agree with each other on only 53--60\% of the decisions, about as often as the proxies agree with the 1.7B reference (Figure~\ref{fig:app_rq02_seed_uncertainty}).
A larger reference does not change the verdict either. Against six configurations trained to 3B, at $K \in \{8, 15, 30, 50\}$, benchmark accuracy ranks the design variants at 0.47--0.53 from every proxy, the 1.7B models included, while per-language BPB reaches 0.81--0.96 on only 10--12 BPB tasks from five of the configurations (Appendix~\ref{app:rq10_size_generalisation}).

\subsection{Anticipation: Can cheap proxy statistics predict decision accuracy?}
\label{subsec:rq3}

\begin{rqfinding}
\textbf{1. No single surrogate leads at every proxy size.} Early-checkpoint ranking agreement and the relative-standard-deviation SNR trade the lead, both with a moderate association with DA-size ($\rho \approx 0.6$) that is already present at 90M.
\\
\\
\textbf{2. The predictive part of SNR is its noise term.} Signal alone and the margin above chance carry no information about decision accuracy on benchmark tasks.
\\
\\
\textbf{3. Pooled correlations hide large differences between benchmarks and languages.} The same SNR tracks decision accuracy closely within HellaSwag but barely on per-language BPB, where the SNR definitions reverse sign at 600M.
\end{rqfinding}


As discussed in Section~\ref{subsec:rq2}, decision accuracy (DA) measures whether the ranking of models with different development decisions agrees with their ordering at reference scale.
\textcolor{blue}{However, DA is inherently retrospective, meaning that computing it requires both the proxy ranking and the ranking of the larger reference models.}
Ideally, we would instead like a \textcolor{blue}{\textit{surrogate metric} that can be computed from the proxy models alone and approximates the DA that would be obtained against the reference.}
Prior work has proposed several such diagnostics, most notably signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) \citep{heineman_signal_2025}, which compares the separation between model variants to the variability of their evaluation scores.
These methods have primarily been studied in English settings and at scales where downstream benchmark signal is already measurable.
In this experiment, we investigate first whether existing surrogate metrics remain predictive of DA in the multilingual setting and second, which surrogate provides a reliable signal at the smallest model scale.

We first test whether statistics proposed to characterize evaluation reliability can serve as surrogates for DA without requiring the reference model.
\textcolor{blue}{For each benchmark-language task and proxy size, we compute each surrogate using only information available at the proxy scale and measure its Spearman ($\rho$) association with DA}, i.e., agreement between the proxy-size ranking and the ranking at the 1.7B reference model.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} shows the Spearman ($\rho$) association with DA of different surrogate implementations across model sizes over all benchmarks that have passed the above-chance threshold, depicting how their predictive strength changes as the proxy scale increases and whether any surrogate remains consistently informative across scales.
We compare several families of surrogate statistics that can be computed without the reference model, including scaling-fit \(R^2\), SNR-based measures \citep{heineman_signal_2025} and their signal/noise components, margin above chance, and early-checkpoint ranking agreement.
Scaling-fit \(R^2\) measures how well benchmark performance follows a log-linear trend across the model sizes available up to the proxy point. Margin above chance measures how far the benchmark score is above random-guessing performance. Early-checkpoint ranking agreement measures how well the ranking of model variants at an early training checkpoint matches their ranking at the final checkpoint of the same model size.
For each benchmark-language task and proxy size, we compute the surrogate using only information available up to that proxy point and measure its Spearman association with DA.
For the definition of the SNR variants, we refer to Appendix~\ref{subsec:methodology-snr} and Appendix~\ref{app:surrogates}.

\textbf{Surrogate predictivity in the multilingual setting.}
We find that \textcolor{blue}{no single surrogate consistently predicts decision accuracy} across the multilingual settings of our model ladder.
Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} shows that among the simple candidates, early-checkpoint ranking agreement and the relative-standard-deviation SNR provide the strongest overall association with DA ($\rho = 0.55$--$0.62$ and $0.54$--$0.61$ across proxy sizes), \textcolor{blue}{suggesting that benchmarks whose ranking settles early in training and whose scores vary little across checkpoints are also more likely to preserve the ranking of model variants across scales.} Scaling-fit \(R^2\) follows at $\rho = 0.42$--$0.46$. On the accuracy tasks alone, without the bBPB twins, the SNR leads at four of the five proxy sizes ($\rho = 0.35$--$0.43$, Appendix~\ref{app:rq04_surrogates}).
For SNR-based surrogates, however, performance depends strongly on how noise is defined, with some noise estimates yielding substantially stronger associations than others. For example, in the catalogue analysis of Appendix~\ref{app:sur-results}, which pools the proxy sizes and reads one point per (benchmark, language) pair, replacing checkpoint-based noise with $k$-fold benchmark noise lowers the association of the relative-standard-deviation SNR with DA from \(\rho=0.79\) to \(\rho=0.53\) (Table~\ref{tab:sur-corr-snr}).
The aggregate results in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3} pool all benchmark-language tasks that pass the chance threshold. When we stratify the analysis by benchmark or multilingual training regime, however, the relationships differ by stratum.
While the strongest SNR association in the pooled catalogue is \(\rho=0.79\), the best SNR definition reaches \(\rho=0.93\) for HellaSwag but only \(\rho=0.70\) on the tasks in the eight languages of the $K = 8$ list and \(\rho=0.62\) for per-language BPB (Table~\ref{tab:sur-corr-strata}).
These values are \(0.14\) higher, \(0.09\) lower, and \(0.17\) lower than the pooled association, respectively.
Table~\ref{tab:sur-corr-strata} (Appendix~\ref{app:sur-results}) gives the surrogate-DA Spearman correlations per benchmark, language tier and proxy size, and Appendix~\ref{app:rq04_surrogates} the per-language correlations of every SNR definition.

\textbf{At what model scale does surrogate signal emerge?}
\textcolor{blue}{We next ask how early in model development a surrogate becomes informative about DA.}
Using the same analysis in Figure~\ref{fig:rq3}, we compare surrogate-DA associations across increasing proxy model sizes, from 90M to 1B.
This allows us to test whether useful surrogate signal is already present at the smallest scale and whether it strengthens or remains stable as model scale increases.
\textcolor{blue}{At the smallest proxy scale, the two leading surrogates already reach a moderate association with DA ($\rho = 0.59$ and $0.56$ at 90M), and neither strengthens consistently as the proxy grows.}
Early-checkpoint ranking agreement is strongest at 175M ($\rho=0.62$), and its association changes little at 350M ($\rho=0.58$), indicating that this early signal is fairly consistent across scales.
By 350M, inverted relative-standard-deviation noise reaches $\rho=0.56$ and discrepancy-based SNR reaches $\rho=0.33$.
Scaling-fit $R^2$ becomes available only from 350M onward, where it reaches $\rho=0.46$ and stays at $\rho = 0.42$--$0.46$ up to 1B.
Overall, a moderate surrogate signal is present from the smallest proxy scale but does not strengthen with model size, and the surrogate providing the strongest association alternates between early-checkpoint agreement and the relative-standard-deviation SNR.
Similar to our earlier findings on multilingual benchmark scaling, we find that decision surrogates behave less consistently than suggested by prior English-focused studies, with their usefulness depending strongly on the benchmark and the training-language regime, and for per-language BPB on the proxy size (Figure~\ref{fig:rq3}, right).

\textbf{What a surrogate cannot see.}
Every surrogate statistic is computed from the proxy runs, so none contains information about the sign of the score difference at the reference size.
The 600M per-language BPB result of \S\ref{subsec:rq2} is the measured instance. Per-language BPB scales almost perfectly with model size (median $R^2 = 1.00$, \S\ref{subsec:rq0}), yet at 600M its DA-size against the 1.7B reference is 0.74, against 0.92--0.96 at the other proxy sizes.
The proxy statistics cannot flag this case, because the disagreement comes from the depth pairs, whose 600M difference is below the seed noise and whose 1.7B difference is only about one seed standard deviation (Table~\ref{tab:app_rq03_flip_attribution}).
At this size the SNR definitions, signal alone and the scaling-fit $R^2$ all turn negative ($\rho = -0.41$ to $-0.64$, Figure~\ref{fig:rq3}, right), so a statistic that ranks the 600M proxy as reliable points to the wrong decisions.
Two follow-ups would test this limit directly: a held-out-language test of the token thresholds of \S\ref{subsec:rq0}, and a separation of within-proxy detectability from cross-scale agreement.
