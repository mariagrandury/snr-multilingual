Part one

The original programme

Seven questions carried over from the Signal-and-Noise framework and adapted to the ladder. Each is one directory under src/signal-and-noise/analysis/, run in dependency order by run_all_predictivity.sh.

RQ0Answered

How do scores move with compute, and which benchmarks are above chance at all?

TL;DR

113 of 461 gated tasks beat chance at some size. It is overwhelmingly an answer-count effect: 42 % of two-option tasks clear it and 8 % of four-option ones.

Setup and method

A (benchmark, size) cell is above random when its mean final score beats chance by a fixed margin. Chance comes from the option counts derived from the evaluated samples in configs/tasks.json, with a per-family fallback.

above_random(b, s)  ⟺  mean_score(b, s) > 1/n_options(b) + 0.05
per-language BPB and generative tasks have no chance level and are never gated

Every downstream SNR cell that fails the gate is set to NaN, so the gate propagates to every other RQ and never depends on one. Script: rq00_acc_vs_flops/above_random.py, curves in run_apertus.py.

Results
Answer optionsChanceClears at some sizeShare
2 — completion, minimal pair0.5080 / 19142 %
3 — XNLI0.3312 / 1867 %
4 — knowledge MCQA0.2521 / 2528 %

Survivors grow monotonically with size: 3 at 90M, 62 at 175M, 71 at 350M, 85 at 600M, 103 at 1B and 110 at 1.7B. Four families never clear chance anywhere — global_mmlu_full, global_piqa_parallel_cloze, global_piqa_nonparallel_cloze and truthfulqa-multi_mc1. At 1B the survivors are multiblimp (57 tasks), hellaswag (15), xnli (9), xwinograd (6), xcopa (6), xstorycloze (5), arc (2), paws (2), include_base_44 (1); the 1.7B rung adds three xnli, two paws, one hellaswag and the first belebele task to clear chance anywhere.

On the earlier 36-model sweep the same four-option families cleared chance for external 270M–70B models (122 of 124), so this is a capability floor of the small rungs and not a property of the benchmarks.

Follow-ups
  • The +0.05 margin is a convention inherited from the framework. A per-task binomial test against chance, using the real sample count, would replace a fixed margin with a stated false-positive rate.
  • The gate has moved from 94 of 324 to 113 of 461 tasks in a week of extra evals. Worth tracking as a time series, since it moves the denominator of every other result.
RQ1Answered

Does a benchmark rank design variants at a small size, or an early checkpoint, the way the reference does?

TL;DR

15,558 decision-accuracy cells over 568 tasks and 15 benchmark families. This is not a finding in itself — it is the ground truth every other RQ is scored against.

Setup and method

Models are the ladder's cells. The cross-size identity is the cell's design variant — language setting, scheme, architecture, seed — so one variant trained at two sizes forms one pair. Two definitions:

DA-size(b; s→S) = share of variant pairs ordered the same at size s and size S, both at their final checkpoint
DA-ckpt(b; f@s) = share ordered the same at checkpoint f ∈ {20, 40, 60, 80} % and the final one, within size s
computed by decision_acc_fast; a pair needs ≥ 2 variants present at both buckets

Multilingual tasks are scored only on cells that train the language, so each task's population is the variants that exist at both sizes and were evaluated on it. n travels with every value. Script: rq01_decision_accuracy/compute_da.py.

Results

4,198 DA-size cells and 11,360 DA-ckpt cells. Six size pairs are computable: 175M→350M, 175M→600M, 175M→1.7B, 350M→600M, 350M→1.7B, 600M→1.7B. Aggregated over languages, the well-powered pairs are the three that stop at 600M, backed by 11 model families; the three reaching 1.7B rest on 2 families each, so their DA can only read 0 or 1 and should not be read as a strong result.

On the 11-family pairs the ordering is clear: training loss 0.91–0.96, per-language BPB 0.75–0.86, and the benchmark families clustered near 0.5.

Follow-ups
  • The 1.7B columns need more finished cells before they mean anything. Two families is one pairwise call.
  • DA is quantised at these counts. A continuous alternative — rank correlation over variants instead of pair agreement — would use the same data with less granularity loss.
RQ2Answered

Which of 22 SNR definitions predicts decision accuracy, and does the choice survive a seed swap?

TL;DR

Best variant overall is discrepancy at r = +0.06 against size decision accuracy, and dist_std at +0.20 against checkpoint decision accuracy, against 0.791 reported on the English DataDecide ladder. Nothing about the choice survives a seed swap: Spearman ρ is +0.01, and per-language family agreement is 11 %.

Setup and method

Twenty-two spread statistics — dispersion, relative-spread, discrepancy, robust and depth families — are computed per (task, size) from the variants' final scores and the late-checkpoint noise, then correlated per language against both DA definitions.

signal(b,s) = spread over design variants' final scores  (22 statistics)
noise(b,s) = σ over the last 5 checkpoints of the reference run, ÷ mean
SNR = signal / noise   score = mean over languages of Pearson r( log₁₀ SNR , DA )

Two technical decisions that changed the answer historically, both recorded in src/signal-and-noise/CLAUDE.md: rank variants by signed Pearson r, never R², because R² conflates +0.5 with −0.5 and floats anti-signals to the top; and apply _is_parent_task, because the subject facets of Global-MMLU are statistical replicates that six-fold-weight whichever variant tracks the subject decomposition.

The seed holdout trains the ranking on seeds 64 and 313 of the ×3 cells and tests it on seed 1904 of the same cells. Scripts: run_apertus_snr_variants.py, compare_seed_splits.py.

Results
Variantr vs DA-sizer vs DA-ckptOverall

The extra eval coverage moved this, and downwards. Successive runs have put the checkpoint-DA leader at +0.20 (iqr), +0.32 (rel_mpsd) and now +0.20 (dist_std), on a different variant each time, and no variant is best on both kinds of decision. That pattern is what an estimate dominated by noise looks like, so the claim to carry forward is the negative one.

Seed holdout. Spearman ρ on the global ranking is +0.01 under checkpoint DA and −0.20 under size DA. Pearson r between splits is −0.06 (n = 1,104) and +0.27 (n = 22). Per-language family agreement is 11 % and 1 % over 122 language cells, and exact-variant agreement 5 % and 1 %.

Earlier runs of the same holdout gave ρ = +0.70 and +0.29. Three values that far apart on the same test are the result: the estimate is noise. The conclusion stable across all of them is the negative one: recommend a variant family, never an exact variant, and never a per-language argmax.

Follow-ups
  • The seed holdout rests on six ×3 cells. It cannot separate "the ranking is unstable" from "the estimate is unstable". See P2.
  • Noise here is late-checkpoint noise. RQ6 shows it understates seed noise by 2×, so every SNR in this table is optimistic by roughly that factor.
RQ3Blocked

Does our SNR agree with AllenAI DataDecide on the tasks both corpora evaluate?

TL;DR

Not run. The DataDecide-side SNR table is a git-lfs pointer in this clone and lfs.github.com is unreachable from the build environment. One git lfs pull where the endpoint resolves and the stage runs unchanged.

Setup and method

Cross-corpus Pearson r and Spearman ρ of log SNR over the English tasks both corpora evaluate, at matched sizes (90M↔90M through 1B↔1B). Script: rq03_allenai_comparison/analyze.py, fed by build_allenai_variants.py.

Worth stating before the number arrives: the shared universe is small — arc_easy, arc_challenge, hellaswag and MMLU through the Global-MMLU English split. DataDecide's signal is a dispersion over 25 data recipes; ours is over language settings and schemes, a narrower axis. A low correlation would say the populations differ before it says the definition does.

Follow-ups
  • Run it from an environment with LFS access. It is the only RQ with no number at all.
  • Because the axes differ, the honest comparison may be a matched-axis one: recompute DataDecide's SNR over a sub-population of its recipes chosen to have similar dispersion to ours.
RQ4Answered

Can a language or subject subset beat the full benchmark's SNR?

TL;DR

Yes, and by a lot on the weakest benchmarks. Median gain +1.09 SNR on Global-MMLU subjects and +0.80 on its per-language split. The largest single gain takes global_piqa_parallel_cloze at 175M from 1.08 to 3.15 on seven of its languages.

Setup and method

For each multilingual family the per-language tasks are ranked by standalone SNR, then cumulative subsets are swept and the best recorded per size, against a random-order baseline. Three cases: per benchmark, Global-MMLU subjects, and Global-MMLU per language. The per-language BPB family is swept the same way — which languages make the sharpest macro average. Script: rq04_smooth_subtasks/smooth_subtasks.py.

RQ5Underpowered

Which benchmark design features predict reliability?

TL;DR

The answer-count penalty lives in the gate, upstream of SNR. Among the nine families that survive it, no design feature is individually significant — option count H = 0.02, p = 0.88; task format H = 1.12, p = 0.57; curation H = 5.12, p = 0.08.

Setup and method

Per-family SNR — the median over that family's per-language tasks at 1B — grouped by curation method, source origin, task format, option count and a reading-passage flag, then tested with a family-level Kruskal–Wallis. Fifteen families carry provenance, including the natively-sourced INCLUDE and Global PIQA and the human-translated IrokoBench families. Script: rq05_benchmark_creation/analyze.py.

  • Testing on survivors only conditions on the outcome. A model over all 15 families with gate status as a covariate would use the discarded information.
RQ6Partly answered

Which proxy sizes rank an intervention the way the reference does, at each language count?

TL;DR

For bits per byte, 350M is enough: it agrees with the reference on the scheme decision at 8, 15 and 30 languages while 175M gets it backwards. Benchmarks now score, and they read the same decision as a coin flip at both proxy rungs, 0.45–0.57 across the three settings. Every comparison still resolves against 600M, not 1B.

Setup and method

Three reads over the (proxy size, L) grid. Script: rq06_proxy_predictivity/analyze.py.

i. Intervention DA. With two levels, pairwise agreement reduces to sign agreement:
  DA(L, s) = share of population items i with sign(xiA−xiB)|s = sign(xiA−xiB)|ref
  populations: bpb_trained (languages both levels train), bpb_all (100 subsets), benchmark, bpb_macro

ii. Scaling-law error. log BPB = a − α log N fitted on the proxy rungs, predicting the reference
  rel_error = (predicted − observed) / observed

iii. Effect vs noise. |Δ| ÷ seed std, and ÷ detrended late-checkpoint std
  detrended because under WSD the final window is still descending

The reference at each L is the largest size trained there. Interventions: depth (deep vs shallow) exists at L ∈ {1, 2}; data scheme (A vs B) at L ∈ {8, 15, 30}.

Results

The scheme decision on bits per byte, over the languages both schemes train:

ProxyL = 8L = 15L = 30
175M1.000.000.04
350M1.000.831.00

The benchmark population is no longer empty, and it is scored at both rungs. Over 2,896 shared benchmark tasks per setting, a 175M proxy agrees with the 600M reference on 0.46 at L8, 0.50 at L15 and 0.57 at L30, and a 350M proxy on 0.45, 0.55 and 0.48. A bigger proxy buys nothing, which is the sharpest contrast with bits per byte, where 350M is the size at which the answer becomes right. Under the strict test, a proxy that agrees and keeps agreeing at every larger size, 3,995 of 8,449 benchmark comparisons pass (47 %) against 365 of 500 for per-language BPB (73 %). That 47 % was 0 % on the thinner ladder, but it is also close to what agreeing at two rungs by chance would give, so read it as unreliable rather than as working.

The two measurements also disagree about the answer itself: bits per byte prefers scheme B at L15 and L30, the benchmark suite prefers scheme A at L30.

Scaling-law error. 505 fits, of which 202 predict a 1B reference and 202 a 1.7B one. Median absolute relative error 0.076 predicting 1.7B at L1, 0.051 predicting 1B at L8, 0.058 predicting 1.7B at L30 and 0.043 predicting 1B at L50, so per-language BPB at the reference is predicted from the proxy rungs to within 4–8 %. L2 is the one outlier at 0.244 against a 600M reference.

Effect against noise. Seed noise is 2.04× detrended late-checkpoint noise over 5,546 cells. Against a seed standard deviation of 0.021 nats on final loss: the across-L range is 11.5×, the scheme difference 2.2×, and the depth difference 1.0× — the same model measured twice. Per individual task the depth effect is larger, median 1.43× with 35 % of 853 cells above 2×, so depth separates somewhere, just not on the metric the ladder is built around.

Follow-ups
  • All 36 intervention comparisons resolve against 600M. The finished cells above 600M are 1B at L ∈ {8, 50} and 1.7B at L ∈ {1, 30}, all deep and scheme A, so none has a matched twin and nothing here is yet a small-to-large result.
  • The 47 % strict-test pass rate for benchmarks needs a null. Shuffling the reference signs would say how much of it is agreement and how much is two coin flips landing the same way.

Part two

How good are our instruments?

Four questions asked of the benchmarks themselves, before any result is quoted with them. All four come from documents/figures/fig_rqs.py on the same published ladder report.

A1Answered

Which benchmarks scale predictably?

TL;DR

Completion tasks do — median R² = 0.97 for LAMBADA-MT and 0.96 for XWinograd and per-language bits per byte. Knowledge tasks barely move with size: Global-MMLU sits at 0.08, INCLUDE at 0.15 and Belebele at 0.31.

Setup and method

Each task's mean score is fitted against log non-embedding parameters across the four healthy rungs — 175M, 350M, 600M, 1B. 90M is excluded because nine of its ten runs diverged. A task needs three of the four rungs to be fitted.

y = b · log₁₀ N + a   fitted by least squares over the sizes present
R² = 1 − SSres / SStot   ρ = Spearman( log₁₀ N , y )
reported as the median over each family's tasks; input is above_random_scores.csv

Sign matters here. Bits per byte and training loss fall as models improve, so ρ = −1 is their ideal, not a failure.

Follow-ups
  • A linear fit in log N is the simplest defensible form at four rungs. The fit still stops at 1B: the two finished 1.7B cells are at L1 and L30, and the 1B cells at L8 and L50, so no design variant yet spans both. Once one does, a saturating form (Bhagia-style loss-to-accuracy) becomes fittable and would separate "not scaling" from "already saturated".
  • The fit is over size at fixed language count. The same fit over compute would fold in the token budget and is the more decision-relevant axis.
A2Answered

Which benchmarks are stable?

TL;DR

Training loss (0.009), xstorycloze (0.010), multiblimp (0.011) and per-language bits per byte (0.014) give the same answer twice. include_base_44 at 0.206 is twenty times noisier than any of them, which compounds A1: it neither moves with size nor sits still.

Setup and method

Relative noise at 1B — the standard deviation over a run's last five saved checkpoints, divided by the mean of those checkpoints. This is the denominator of every SNR quoted in this document.

noise_rel_std(b, s) = σt ∈ last 5( ct ) / c̄
checkpoints are taken on the shared grid, so the window is the same fraction of training at every rung

The shared checkpoint grid matters. Without it the five-checkpoint window spans 50 % of a 20-checkpoint run and 12.5 % of a 40-checkpoint one, and the 1B rung would look artificially quiet.

Results

Median relative noise per family at 1B: loss 0.009, xstorycloze 0.010, multiblimp 0.011, bpb 0.014, hellaswag 0.016, xwinograd 0.017, xcopa 0.018, arc 0.021, xnli 0.022, paws 0.023, lambada_openai_mt 0.031, include_base_44 0.206.

Stability alone is not a virtue — a benchmark stuck at chance is perfectly stable. It only becomes meaningful next to the signal, which is A4.

Follow-ups
  • This is checkpoint noise. RQ6 shows seed noise is 2.04× larger, so the honest per-family stability ranking should be recomputed on seed replicates once more ×3 cells exist.
  • Noise is measured on the reference run only. Per-family noise may itself depend on size and language count, which this single number hides.
A3Answered

Which languages benefit most from increasing model scale?

TL;DR

All 100 validation languages improve, median 0.391 bits per byte. The ordering is the result: Spearman ρ = +0.63 between where a language starts and what it gains, so scale helps most where the model is currently worst.

Setup and method

On the 50-language mixture, deep architecture, scheme A, seed 1904, at each language's final checkpoint:

baselineℓ = BPBℓ(175M)     gainℓ = BPBℓ(175M) − BPBℓ(1B)
L50 is used because it is the only setting with a complete 175M→1B chain

The three patterns the team named map onto the plot's regions, split at the median of each axis rather than an absolute floor, because nothing regresses. Bits per byte is byte-normalised, so it is comparable across languages with different tokenizer fertility — that is why it is the metric here rather than perplexity.

A4Answered

Does the benchmark distinguish the things we want to compare?

TL;DR

Bits per byte clears its own noise at 7.1×, and it is no longer alone: hellaswag reaches 5.1× on 15 tasks and multiblimp 2.3× on 57. Six families are still below 1×, meaning they vary more between checkpoints of one model than between the models we are trying to tell apart.

Setup and method

Signal is the spread across the design variants at a fixed size; noise is the spread within one run. Both are relative, so the ratio is dimensionless and comparable across benchmarks with different score scales.

signal_rel_std(b,s) = σvariants( final score ) / mean
noise_rel_std(b,s) = σlast 5 ckpts( score ) / mean
ratio = signal / noise   this ratio is the rel_std SNR variant; reported at 1B

Part three

Can a cheap measurement stand in for the real one?

The three questions the ladder exists to answer. A proxy is only worth having if the decision it produces is the decision the expensive model would have produced.

B1Answered

How early can we predict final multilingual performance?

TL;DR

Bits per byte calls the ranking at 20 % of training and never improves on it — 0.91 agreement, flat to 80 %. Four fifths of every run adds nothing to the ranking. The benchmark median climbs slowly from 0.65 to 0.80 and only hellaswag (0.90) and arc (0.88) are as early as bits per byte.

Setup and method

Within one size, the checkpoint nearest each fraction of the run is compared against that same run's final checkpoint. This is DA-ckpt from RQ1, read across the fractions rather than aggregated.

DA-ckpt(b; f@s) = share of variant pairs ordered the same at fraction f and at the end
f ∈ {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8}   da_early_fracs in the snr section of configs/models.json

Reported at 1B, the largest size with a full checkpoint grid. The benchmark families are drawn individually and as a median, rather than in six invented colours, because they are one kind of thing and the contrast against bits per byte is the point.

Results

The benchmark spread is enormous at every fraction, 0.00 to 1.00 at 20 % and 0.50 to 1.00 at 80 %, because most families have a handful of comparable tasks and the metric is quantised at that count. Bits per byte is flat because it is measured over 101 subsets and genuinely does not change its mind.

The practical consequence is a budget one. If the decision is read on bits per byte, the milestone-eval rule could fire far earlier than it does and free most of the evaluation budget.

Follow-ups
  • This is agreement with a run's own final ranking, not with a larger model. Early-and-small is the combination that actually saves money, and B3 only covers the small half.
  • Under WSD the last 20 % is the decay phase. Whether 20 % of a run with a different schedule is equally decisive is untested.
B2Answered

What is the smallest evaluation suite that reliably predicts full multilingual model quality?

TL;DR

Four tasks reach ρ = 0.98 against the bits-per-byte ranking. The whole suite of 2,897 reaches 0.53. A small curated suite is not a compromise here, it is strictly better than running everything.

Setup and method

The reference is the ranking of models by their mean per-language bits per byte, sign-flipped so higher is better throughout. Benchmark tasks are added best-first, judged by how well each alone tracks that reference, and the growing suite is scored the same way.

refm = −meanℓ BPBm,ℓ   m over the 41 models of the canonical pool
solo(t) = Spearman( score·,t , ref )  → tasks sorted descending
suite(k) = Spearman( mean of z-scored top-k tasks , ref )
z-scored per task so tasks on different scales contribute equally; a task must be scored on ≥ 60 % of models to enter

The z-scoring is the one substantive choice. Without it the average is dominated by whichever tasks have the widest raw range, which is a scale artefact rather than a signal.

Results
SuiteTasksSpearman ρ vs the BPB ranking
Best single task10.96
Best suite40.98
Within 10 % of the peak1 – 270.88 – 0.98
Everything2,8970.53

2,893 of the 2,897 suite sizes beat the whole suite. The decline is monotone past the plateau and the cause is mechanical: the tail is at-chance tasks whose scores are noise, and averaging noise into a signal dilutes it. This is the strongest practical result in the compendium — it says the evaluation budget is being spent on tasks that make the answer worse.

Follow-ups
  • The suite is selected and evaluated on the same 41 models, so ρ = 0.98 is optimistic. A seed or size holdout would give the honest number and say whether the same four tasks are chosen twice.
  • Best-first is greedy and ignores redundancy. A selection that rewards complementary tasks would likely find a better four.
  • The reference is bits per byte, which assumes BPB is model quality. It is the best available proxy on this ladder but it is not a downstream task.
B3Answered

Which is the most informative surrogate?

TL;DR

Per-language bits per byte, on 73 % of 500 comparisons, and 175M alone suffices for a third of them. Benchmarks on 47 % of 8,449, up from 0 % on the thinner ladder. The two aggregate metrics look better but rest on five comparisons each.

Setup and method

This is the min-model-size question the team sketched, aggregated across language counts. A surrogate works for a given measurement when some proxy size picks the reference's winner and every larger size keeps picking it — the monotonicity requirement is what makes it a usable rule rather than a coincidence.

min_predictive(x) = smallest s such that ∀ s′ ≥ s : sign(Δx)|s′ = sign(Δx)|ref
NaN when no such size exists; reference is the largest size trained at that L

Only the second and last rows are well powered. Training loss and macro bits per byte are single measurements, so five comparisons each, and should not be ranked against the others on this evidence. The comparison that matters is 73 % against 47 %, and the benchmark figure deserves a null before it is called a success: agreeing at two rungs by chance lands near 47 % on its own.

Broken out by language count, per-language bits per byte fails on 50 of 100 subsets at L1 — where the intervention is depth, the axis that sits at the seed-noise floor — and on 15 to 24 at every other setting. Benchmarks pass on 38 of 73 at L1, 81 of 141 at L2, 1,160 of 2,740 at L8, 1,458 of 2,749 at L15 and 1,258 of 2,746 at L30, a flat 42–53 % at every language count.

Follow-ups
  • Only two proxy rungs exist below the reference. The curve the team sketched needs four or five, and a reference above them, before it can be drawn.
  • The monotonicity requirement is strict. Reporting the share that agree at a given size, without requiring every larger size to agree, would be a softer and possibly more useful rule.

Part four

What we should also be asking

Five questions the current programme does not cover. Each is answerable with the ladder we already have, or with a clearly bounded addition to it. Seven further proposals, narrower and already written up, are in §9 of the report.

P1Proposed

What is the cheapest evaluation that still gets the decision right?

TL;DR

B1, B2 and B3 each cut one axis in isolation — checkpoint, suite, size. Nobody has cut all three at once, and the savings multiply. The three results together imply a 20 % checkpoint of a 350M model on four tasks, and that combination has never been tested.

Why it is missing

Each of the three answers holds the other two axes at their most generous setting. B1 reads bits per byte at full suite and full size; B2 uses final checkpoints of every model; B3 uses full runs. Interactions are plausible in both directions: a four-task suite may be far noisier at 20 % of training than at the end, or the size reduction may be free once the suite is curated.

Proposed method
for (s, f, k) in sizes × fractions × suite_sizes:
  DA(s, f, k) = agreement with the reference decision at (largest size, final ckpt, full suite)
  cost(s, f, k) ≈ Ctrain(s) · f  +  Ceval · k · nckpts(f)
report the Pareto front of (cost, DA), not the grid

Everything needed is already in the ladder report — the grid is a re-slice of existing DA machinery, not new training. The deliverable is one curve a practitioner can read a budget off, plus the single recommended triple.

What would make it decisive
  • Report it separately for the depth and scheme decisions. The two axes have very different effect sizes and the cheapest adequate evaluation almost certainly differs.
  • Include the cost of the reference itself. If the proxy costs 30 % of the reference, the saving is smaller than the DA table suggests.
P2Proposed

How many seeds does a per-language claim actually need?

TL;DR

Every negative result here — the SNR ranking not transferring, depth sitting at the noise floor, per-language argmax at 10 % agreement — is consistent with "the effect is absent" and with "six replicate cells cannot see it". We have never computed which.

Why it is missing

The whole noise estimate rests on six ×3 cells: 175M and 600M at L ∈ {1, 2, 50}. That is enough to say seed noise is 2.04× checkpoint noise, and not enough to put an interval on any per-language quantity. RQ2's holdout swung from ρ = +0.70 to +0.29 on one night of extra evals, which is the symptom.

Proposed method

A straightforward power analysis on the quantities already computed, then a bootstrap over the replicates we have:

σseed(ℓ, s) from the ×3 cells  →  nrequired(ℓ) = ⌈ 2 (zα/2+zβ)² σ² / δ² ⌉
δ = the smallest effect worth detecting; the scheme effect (2.2×σ) and the depth effect (1.0×σ) are the two candidates

Then resample the existing seeds to show how the holdout ρ and the per-language agreement behave as a function of replicate count, which turns "the estimate is noisy" into a number.

What would make it decisive
  • Every negative claim in this document should carry a minimum detectable effect. Without one, "no significant difference" is unreadable.
  • If the required n is large, that is itself the finding, and it argues for spending the next node-hours on replicates rather than on new cells.
P3Proposed

Can we build a benchmark where the signal actually is?

TL;DR

The suite now reaches 62 of the 68 languages we can score, but in 40 of them the only surviving benchmark is MultiBLiMP, and no knowledge or reasoning benchmark clears the gate in any language at all. The coverage problem has become a construct problem: we can measure grammaticality almost everywhere and everything else nowhere.

Why it is missing

This came out of RQ2 as a description. Nobody has turned it into a construction. It is still the most actionable finding the project has produced, and the refresh sharpened it rather than removing it: ten families survive the gate somewhere, but global_mmlu_full, both Global-PIQA variants and truthfulqa-multi_mc1 survive nowhere, so the whole knowledge and reasoning half of the suite is silent at every size we have trained.

Proposed method
  1. Rank the 87 uncovered languages by BPB SNR at 1B. The top 39 clear 10.0 and are the target list.
  2. For each, check what the harness already has that the gate rejected, and why — no task at all, or a task that never beats chance.
  3. Pick the format the ladder shows survives at these sizes: two-option, completion or minimal pair. A4 and RQ5 agree that four-option knowledge MCQA is dead at this scale.
  4. Validate a candidate by the same gate plus signal ratio, not by human agreement alone.
What would make it decisive
  • MultiBLiMP already covers grammaticality in many of these languages cheaply and is the steadiest family on the ladder. Extending it may be far cheaper than new authoring, even though its signal is currently low.
  • The anti-correlation may be partly mechanical: high-resource languages are well served by every mixture because they are high-resource. Stating that mechanism is part of the result, not a caveat against it.
P4Proposed

Is the optimizer timescale quietly depressing every small proxy?

TL;DR

AdEMAMix's slow average is fixed at 10,000 steps while run length spans 18× across the ladder. The 90M rung is shorter than its own optimizer's memory and nine of ten runs diverged. The control run says the fix also beats the uncorrected 175M, which means the next rung up is depressed too.

Why it is missing

It was diagnosed as a 90M problem and handled by dropping the rung. But the evidence points further up: a 90M run with β₃ tied to run length reaches final loss 2.778, against 5.762 uncorrected, and that corrected 90M beats the uncorrected 175M. If 175M is depressed, every "the small proxy disagrees with the reference" result in this document is partly an artefact of the proxy being handicapped rather than small.

That would be a finding with reach beyond this project. Step-fixed optimizer hyperparameters are standard practice, and small-model proxies are how the field makes decisions.

Proposed method
β₃ ← tied to run length (0.2 × total steps), holding everything else fixed
cells: 175M and 350M at L ∈ {1, 2, 8}, one seed
read: Δ final loss, Δ per-language BPB, and Δ intervention DA against the same reference

Six runs. The comparison that matters is not whether the corrected models are better — they will be — but whether the corrected proxy's decision agrees with the reference more often than the uncorrected one does.

What would make it decisive
  • If DA improves, every proxy result needs re-reading and the ladder needs re-running with the tie. If DA is unchanged, the rung is simply worse and nothing else follows, which is also worth knowing.
  • Check the same question for the learning-rate law and the warmup, both of which are also defined against a run's own budget.
P5Proposed

Would the decision change if we compared at equal compute instead of equal size?

TL;DR

Every result here compares a small model at its own full budget against a large one at its full budget. A practitioner has a fixed budget and chooses shape. Those two framings can disagree, and the ladder can already answer the second.

Why it is missing

Carried over from the report's §9 and still unrun. The ladder trains every rung to 100 × N tokens, so size and compute move together and the current design cannot separate them. But the IsoFLOP slices exist: at 1e19, 3.2e19 and 1e20 FLOPs, three to four sizes each have a checkpoint on the shared grid.

Proposed method
compute = 6 · (Nnon-emb + d·V) · D   already a column in the ladder report
for each IsoFLOP slice: pick each size's nearest checkpoint, then run the RQ6 intervention DA
compare against the equal-size, equal-fraction answer

No new training. It re-slices checkpoints already evaluated, using the compute column the FLOPs convention fix put on a single basis.

What would make it decisive
  • If the two framings pick different proxies, the practitioner-facing recommendation has to be stated at fixed budget, and the paper's framing changes with it.
  • The milestone-eval rule needed for clean IsoFLOP slices is decided but not implemented, so today's slices use nearest checkpoints, which adds error the comparison must carry.
